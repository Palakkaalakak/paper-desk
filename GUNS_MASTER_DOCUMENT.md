# Part I — Introduction, Trading Station Setup & Watchlist Preparation

## Introduction to Day Trading, Setting Up Your Trading Station & Preparing Your Watchlist

### What GUNS Is, and Why It Is a Different Animal From Swing Trading

Everything the course has covered up to this point has lived on the daily chart. Positions were built over days or weeks, entries were planned with the luxury of an evening to think about them, and the trader's edge came from reading trend structure that unfolded slowly enough to be judged at leisure. GUNS throws that entire tempo away. GUNS stands for Gap Up News Scalp, and it is a day trading strategy, which means every position it produces is opened and closed inside a single session, usually inside the first hour after the market opens, and very often inside a single minute. Where swing trading asks the trader to be patient and to tolerate multi-day drawdown while a thesis plays out, GUNS asks for the opposite skill set: the ability to recognize a narrow, repeatable pattern in real time, to act on it within seconds, and to be completely out of the position — win, loss, or scratch — before the coffee has gone cold.

The raw material for GUNS is scarcity. There are roughly seven thousand listed stocks trading in the United States on any given day, and on any given day only a small fraction of them are actually worth a day trader's attention — the course puts that number at under ten. What separates those handful of stocks from the other 6,990 is a news catalyst violent enough to move the stock sharply before the opening bell. A company might report earnings that beat expectations by a wide margin, or it might win a patent dispute, or a drug it is developing might clear a clinical trial, or the Food and Drug Administration might approve one of its products. Whatever the specific trigger, the effect is the same: traders who see the news want a piece of the stock immediately, and because this buying pressure often begins before the exchange has even opened for the day, it shows up first in the pre-market session as a gap — a jump in price with no trading in between the previous close and the new pre-market level. A stock might close a normal trading day near fifteen dollars, and then, after hours, announce earnings that beat expectations enough to send the shares up several tens of percent; by the time the market reopens the next morning, the stock is no longer trading anywhere near fifteen dollars, it has gapped straight up to a materially higher level, and it is that gap, and the momentum behind it, that GUNS exists to exploit.

The mechanics of a GUNS trade are almost the opposite of the mechanics you have practiced on the daily chart. Instead of a signal that takes shape over several candles and gives you time to plan an entry, a GUNS setup announces itself over a handful of one-minute or five-minute candles and demands an entry within seconds of triggering. Instead of holding through the day's noise for a multi-day target, GUNS scalps for a fixed, modest reward — often just a matter of tens of cents per share — and is happy to book that reward and move on to the next opportunity within one or two minutes of getting in. The course's own worked examples make the point vividly. In one case, a stock's pre-market chart showed a clean uptrend on the one-minute candle with a pre-market high sitting at twenty-nine dollars; the plan was to buy one cent above that high, at 29.01, with a stop loss roughly fifty cents below entry and a profit target set at twice the risk, meaning a stop of roughly thirty cents would be paired with a target of roughly sixty cents. When the market opened and price cleared the pre-market high, the order triggered without a hitch and the profit target was hit within a single minute — a full, complete round trip, entry to exit, in sixty seconds. A second example, a stock that pulled back after an initial burst of buying interest and then broke above the high of its own pullback candle at 33.39, used a tighter stop just below the pullback candle's low at 33.14 — about twenty cents of risk — against a profit target around forty to fifty cents higher, at roughly 34.01; that trade also hit its target in well under a minute, in that case in about fifty seconds. These are not isolated exceptions. This kind of scalp, entry to exit inside sixty to ninety seconds for a payoff that is roughly double the risk taken, is the daily bread and butter of the strategy, and the course describes GUNS as one of the most consistently profitable strategies it trades, worked on a regular Monday-through-Friday basis.

Because the timeframe is compressed to seconds rather than days, GUNS imposes requirements on the trader's setup that swing trading never did. The first requirement is data. A day trader cannot work from end-of-day charts or delayed quotes; the entire strategy depends on watching price tick by tick as the pre-market session unfolds and as the opening bell approaches, so the platform in use has to provide live, streaming data, and its charting package has to be capable of displaying pre-market activity specifically — the trading that happens in the hours before the official 9:30 a.m. Eastern open, which behaves like a private preview of the day's action, comparable to a store letting its most eager customers in for a closed-door sale before it opens its doors to everyone else. The second requirement is speed of execution. A swing trader placing an order has the luxury of typing in an entry price, a share count, a stop loss, and a profit target one field at a time and taking a couple of minutes to do it. A GUNS trader does not have that luxury: the setup that matters might exist for only a few seconds, and if the order is not away within that window the trade is simply gone. The solution the course teaches is the hot button — a pre-configured order template that has already had its share size, its stop-loss distance, and its target distance baked in ahead of time, so that when the moment to act arrives, the entire order, complete with bracketed stop and target, is submitted with a single click.

There are also two structural, regulatory realities that any trader working this strategy in the United States has to plan around before ever placing a trade. The first is the Securities and Exchange Commission's pattern day trader rule, which requires a minimum account balance of twenty-five thousand US dollars to day trade without restriction; buying and selling the same security within the same trading day makes you a pattern day trader in the SEC's eyes, and if your account balance is below that twenty-five-thousand-dollar threshold while you execute more than three such round trips within a rolling five-day window, your broker is required to freeze your account from further day trading for a period of time. The course's practical recommendation is to keep a buffer above the legal minimum — funding the account with at least thirty thousand dollars rather than exactly twenty-five thousand, since any dip below the threshold, even briefly, triggers the freeze. For traders who genuinely cannot commit that much capital, the course mentions two alternative paths, both flagged with real caution. One is to trade through a broker that operates outside SEC jurisdiction and therefore is not bound by the twenty-five-thousand-dollar rule; the course names one such broker, sureshotrader.com, but is explicit that the instructor has never personally used it, does not recommend it, and is only passing along that some traders use this kind of workaround — anyone considering it is told plainly to look into it and proceed entirely at their own risk. The other alternative, available to traders outside the United States, is to trade Contracts for Difference, or CFDs, which track the underlying stock's price without involving direct share ownership and are therefore not subject to the pattern-day-trader rule; the course notes that CFDs are usable by traders based in Australia, Singapore, and Hong Kong, among other jurisdictions, but are not available to US citizens.

The second structural reality is about hardware rather than regulation. Because a GUNS trader is typically watching several charts on several different timeframes at once — a daily chart for broader context, a five-minute chart for the pre-market setup, and a one-minute chart for the live execution, alongside a scanner and a Level 2 quote window — the course strongly prefers a two-monitor setup, or more than two if the trader can manage it, over a single screen. Everything is technically still possible on one monitor, but constantly resizing and shuffling windows to see what needs to be seen costs precious seconds that a day trader does not have to spare. Beyond the screens themselves, the course is candid that the last ingredient cannot be bought or configured: fast reaction time. That is a skill, not a setting, and it comes only from repetition and practice, which is exactly why the remainder of this chapter walks through the trading station build and the daily preparation ritual in such granular, step-by-step detail — so that by the time a live setup appears on screen, everything except the trader's own reaction has already been decided in advance.

### Building the GUNS Trading Station on Interactive Brokers or Halifax Australia

The trading platform used throughout this course is Interactive Brokers, and everything demonstrated on it applies identically to Halifax Australia, since Halifax runs on the same underlying Trader Workstation software as Interactive Brokers. When either platform is first opened, two tabs exist by default: Classic TWS, referred to as the Trader Workstation, and Mosaic, the newer Mosaic Workstation layout. Neither of these default tabs is set up to trade GUNS. The workflow the course builds from scratch adds five purpose-built tabs on top of them, each one serving a distinct role in the daily routine, and the tabs are meant to be built once and then reused every trading day. It is worth naming all five up front so the logic of the build is clear before diving into the mechanics of constructing each one: Guns News, Guns Charts M5, Guns Chart M1 (in two variants), and Guns Trade.

#### Tab One: Guns News

The Guns News tab is the first stop of the trading day, the tab used to assemble the day's watchlist by reviewing the specific news catalyst behind each candidate stock. It needs exactly four elements arranged on screen at once: a daily candle chart, a five-minute candle chart, a company-specific news feed, and a quotes watchlist. To build it, the trader clicks the plus sign in the tab bar, names the new tab — the course names its own "Guns News" — and clicks Create. From there, the first chart is added through New Window, then Charts; a second chart is added the same way, and the two are resized so that each occupies roughly equal space on screen. Beneath or beside those charts, New Window is used again, this time selecting News and then the company-specific news option, and that panel is stretched to take up about half of the remaining space. Finally, New Window is used once more, this time to add Quotes, then Watchlist, and that panel is given roughly the other half of the remaining space, so that the four elements — two charts, news, and watchlist — each occupy a clear quarter of the tab.

Once the four panels exist, the two charts need their timeframes set. The first is configured as a daily chart, and the course's specific navigation is to click through the chart's own settings — going to File and then the chart configuration — to select a chart that displays roughly six months of data at daily candle resolution. The second chart is set to a two-day span at five-minute candle resolution, which is the interval used to inspect the pre-market session in detail. The two charts inside this tab are then linked together by assigning them to the same color-coded link group — the course uses group four in its own build, though any consistent group number will do — so that typing a new ticker symbol into either chart updates both simultaneously, since both charts inside this tab are meant to always be looking at the same stock. Once everything is arranged and linked the way the trader wants, the layout is locked in place via the Lock Layout control, which prevents accidental resizing or panel movement during live use.

#### Tab Two: Guns Charts M5

The second tab, Guns Charts M5, exists to monitor several candidate stocks at once during the run-up to the market open, each on a five-minute candle. It is built the same way as the first tab's charts — a new tab is created via the plus sign, named, and four separate chart windows are added through New Window and Charts, arranged in an even two-by-two or similarly balanced grid so all four occupy equal space. Unlike the Guns News tab, the four charts here are deliberately unlinked from one another, since the entire point of this tab is to watch four different stocks side by side rather than four views of the same one; each chart's link setting is changed to "No Group." Every chart is then set to a two-day span at five-minute resolution. If the platform's own hot-button panel appears cluttering the chart at this stage, it is removed from view using the panel's own View menu, simply so it does not create visual confusion before hot buttons have actually been configured — that configuration comes later in the build. Once the four charts are unlinked, set to five minutes, and cleaned up visually, the layout is locked.

#### Tab Three: Guns Chart M1 (and Its Companion)

The third tab is built in two versions, and the course frames the choice between them as a matter of personal preference and experience level rather than a hard rule. The first version, called Guns Chart M1, mirrors the structure of the five-minute tab exactly, except that all four charts are set to a one-minute candle resolution rather than five minutes, again with each chart unlinked from the others so four separate stocks can be tracked simultaneously at the fastest resolution the trader watches before execution. The second version, referred to as Guns Chart M1 Part 2, strips that layout down to just two charts rather than four, both still on one-minute candles, and this leaner version is what the course actually recommends a beginner build and use, since trying to watch four stocks simultaneously on the fastest chart timeframe is, in the instructor's own words, more attention than a beginner should try to split; the course's stated personal habit even at an experienced level is to actually trade only one or two stocks at a time despite having four charts available to scan. A beginner following this course is told they may skip building the four-chart one-minute version entirely at first and rely only on the two-chart version until more screen-reading experience is banked.

Mechanically, both versions are built exactly the way the five-minute tab was built: a new tab is created and named, the appropriate number of chart windows is added through New Window and Charts and arranged evenly, each chart is set to a one-minute candle resolution — the course notes this can be selected either as "two days, one minute" or as "today, one minute," both work — every chart's link group is set to "No Group" so the charts stay independent of one another, and the layout is locked once everything is arranged.

#### Tab Four: Guns Trade

The fourth and final tab, Guns Trade, is the one actually used to execute trades once a stock has been chosen from the earlier stages, and it is built differently from the other three because it is not meant to watch several candidates — it is meant to give total command over a single stock across every timeframe that matters, plus the two additional tools needed to read live order flow. This tab contains, in total, three charts of the same stock at three different resolutions — a large one-minute chart, a five-minute chart, and a daily chart — plus a Level 2 market depth window and a live scanner. The one-minute chart is deliberately the largest element on the tab, occupying roughly half the available screen space, because it is the chart the trader is actually watching in the instant of execution; the remaining half of the tab is then divided among the five-minute chart, the daily chart, the Level 2 window, and the scanner.

To build it, a new tab is created and named — the course's own demo calls it "Guns Trade" — and the first chart window added is set to occupy the top or larger half of the tab and configured to "today, one minute." The remaining space is then split to fit two more chart windows plus the Level 2 window and the scanner, requiring some careful dragging and resizing, which the course describes candidly as somewhat fiddly to get exactly even but entirely achievable with patience. The Level 2 quotes window is added via New Window and the Level 2 quotes option; reading what this window actually shows — the stacked bid and ask order book, referred to elsewhere in the course as market depth — is deferred to a later lesson dedicated specifically to interpreting Level 2 data, since it is a skill in its own right.

The scanner used inside this tab is Interactive Brokers' own Mosaic Market Scanner, and it is configured live, from scratch, as part of the build, because this scanner is what will surface tradeable stocks in real time on the actual trading day. To configure it, the trader creates a new custom scan and works through the following criteria in order, exactly as specified in the course:

| Scanner field | Setting used in the course |
|---|---|
| Universe | Stocks, US market |
| Exchanges included | NYSE, ARCA, NASDAQ, NASDAQ BX ("Bats") |
| Instrument type | Corporations only (ETFs and other non-corporate instruments excluded) |
| Last price | Minimum $1.50; maximum set arbitrarily high, e.g. $1,000, effectively meaning no real ceiling |
| Percentage change | Minimum 5%; maximum set arbitrarily high, e.g. 1,000%, effectively meaning no real ceiling |
| Volume | Minimum 30,000 shares of current volume traded (not average daily volume) |
| Sort order | Highest volume to lowest volume |

Once every field is entered, the scan is saved and given a name — the course's own is "Guns Scan" — and the saved scan is dropped into the empty scanner slot inside the Guns Trade tab. From the moment the market begins pre-market trading or opens for the regular session, this scanner runs continuously and automatically surfaces, in real time, any stock that clears all of those thresholds at once: priced at $1.50 or higher, up 5% or more against the previous close, and already trading at least 30,000 shares of volume, sorted so the highest-volume qualifying stocks appear first.

With the scanner and Level 2 window placed, the three price charts inside this tab are configured: the five-minute chart is set to a two-day span at five-minute resolution, the daily chart is set to six months of data at daily resolution, and — critically, unlike the unlinked charts in the M5 and M1 tabs — all three charts inside Guns Trade are linked together into the same color group, so that typing in one ticker symbol changes all three charts simultaneously, since the entire purpose of this tab is to view one single stock from three angles at once while deciding whether and how to enter. The course demonstrates this by typing in a ticker and watching all three linked charts update together. Once the three charts are linked and configured, the tab's layout is locked, completing the full four-tab GUNS trading station: Guns News for building the day's watchlist, Guns Charts M5 for pre-market monitoring of several candidates at five-minute resolution, Guns Chart M1 for the same monitoring at one-minute resolution, and Guns Trade as the execution cockpit for whichever single stock the trader has ultimately chosen to trade.

#### Applying Moving Averages Across Every Chart

Every chart in every tab needs the same set of four moving averages applied before the station is trading-ready, and the course specifies these precisely: a 9-period exponential moving average, a 20-period exponential moving average, a 50-period simple moving average, and a 200-period simple moving average. Rather than configuring these four studies separately on every single chart across all four tabs, the course teaches building them once and then propagating that configuration everywhere else through a saved chart template.

The moving averages are added by going to the chart's Edit menu, then Studies, then Moving Averages, and selecting two instances of the exponential moving average study and two instances of the simple moving average study. The first exponential moving average is set to a period of 9 and the second to a period of 20; the first simple moving average is set to a period of 50 and the second to a period of 200. Once all four are applied, each is given a distinct color and, for the two simple moving averages, a heavier line weight so they stand out as the more significant, longer-term levels: the 9 EMA is colored red (the course notes it defaults to purple and is changed to red via right-click and "Change Indicator Color"), the 50 SMA is colored blue and its line thickness increased to a heavier weight of two via right-click, and the 200 SMA is likewise given a distinct color and the same increased thickness. With all four moving averages styled the way the trader wants, the chart's settings are saved as a reusable template through File, then "Save Chart Template," under a name such as "Chart M1."

From that point forward, every other chart across every tab is brought into line simply by opening that same saved template on each one — dragging a chart out to a size large enough to expose its File menu if the panel is too small to show the menu bar directly, then going to File, "Open Chart Template," and selecting the saved template. This is repeated across every remaining chart in the Guns News tab, both in the daily and five-minute positions, and across all four charts in Guns Charts M5, and across all four (or two) charts in Guns Chart M1, and across the three charts inside Guns Trade — in every case the moving-average styling is loaded from the single saved template rather than rebuilt from scratch, and in every case the chart's own timeframe (daily, five-minute, or one-minute, as appropriate for that particular tab) is re-set immediately afterward, since loading a chart template carries over the study settings but not necessarily the timeframe.

#### Duplicating the Setup on Think or Swim

The course's instructor also maintains a parallel, near-identical set of charts on Think or Swim, a second broker's platform, run on a separate physical computer positioned beside the Interactive Brokers machine. This is explicitly presented as optional and a matter of personal preference rather than a requirement of the strategy, but it is worth understanding why the instructor bothers: Think or Swim's charting is considered visually nicer than Interactive Brokers' own charts, and Think or Swim provides free real-time streaming data as long as the account is funded with at least $3,000, whereas the instructor's actual order execution and hot buttons remain on Interactive Brokers because its execution and hot-button tools are preferred for that purpose. In practice this means two computers side by side: the left-hand machine running Think or Swim purely for viewing five-minute and one-minute charts, and the right-hand machine running Interactive Brokers for both viewing charts and, more importantly, placing and managing every actual trade.

Recreating the moving-average template on Think or Swim follows the same underlying logic even though the exact menu path differs slightly from platform to platform. Starting from a blank chart, the trader opens the Studies editor, adds one exponential moving average set to a period of 9 in a pink or purple color, adds a second exponential moving average set to a period of 20 in red, adds a 50-day simple moving average in blue with a thicker line style, and adds a 200-day simple moving average, also in a thick line style, described in the course as red. Once all four are applied and styled, the chart is saved as a study template named "Guns" via "Save As." Loading this template onto any future chart is then just a matter of going to Studies, then "Load Study," and selecting "Guns."

Think or Swim's own multi-chart layout tool, called the Flexible Grid, is used to build the four-chart, five-minute monitoring view equivalent to the Interactive Brokers M5 tab: opening the Flexible Grid's customize-grid option lets the trader delete unwanted chart panes and add new ones to the right or to the bottom of an existing pane until four independent charts sit in an even grid. Each of the four charts then has the "Guns" study template loaded onto it individually through Studies, then Load, then Guns, and each is set to a five-minute interval; the whole arrangement is then saved as its own layout, named something like "Guns M5" or "Guns Five Minutes," so it can be recalled instantly on any future trading day without rebuilding it from scratch. The same process, with one-minute intervals substituted for five-minute intervals, produces the Think or Swim equivalent of the one-minute monitoring tab.

#### Configuring Hot Buttons for One-Click Execution

The single most consequential piece of the entire trading-station build is the hot-button system, because it is what makes it possible to enter a trade — with its share size, entry price, stop loss, and profit target all correct — inside the one or two seconds that a live GUNS setup actually allows. A hot button is a saved order template attached directly to a chart; clicking it does not open an order-entry form to be filled in, it submits a fully specified bracket order immediately, calculated relative to wherever the trader clicks on the price chart.

Hot buttons are created through the chart's own button panel. The path the course specifies is Edit, then "Global Chart Configuration," then Charts, then Buttons — inside that global chart configuration window, a running list of previously created buttons is displayed, and clicking "New," then "Buy," starts the creation of a new one. The auto-generated label is cleared out and replaced with a custom name that directly describes the button's stop-loss distance — the course's own naming convention is literally "Buy 15 cents," "Buy 20 cents," "Buy 25 cents," and so on up through "Buy 60 cents," each name stating in cents how far below the entry price that particular button's stop loss will sit. The course recommends building a full ladder of these buttons at 5-cent (or similar) increments from roughly 15 cents up to 60 cents, stopping around 60 cents because building buttons past that point starts to produce more buttons than comfortably fit on screen at once.

Configuring any individual button requires four decisions, and the course walks through the arithmetic behind the first of them in detail because it is the one place where risk management is baked directly into the button itself rather than decided trade by trade. The four decisions are as follows.

First is position size, entered under the Size field as an absolute number of shares. The number of shares for a given stop-loss-distance button is derived from the trader's fixed dollar risk per trade divided by that button's own stop-loss distance in cents. The course's worked example assumes a trading account of $10,000 with a risk budget of 1% of capital per trade, which works out to a maximum acceptable loss of $100 on any single trade (1% of $10,000). For a button whose stop loss sits 15 cents below entry, the position size is $100 divided by $0.15, which comes out to 666 shares (the course notes this is not a round number but uses it anyway). For a button whose stop loss sits 20 cents below entry, the same $100 risk divided by $0.20 gives 500 shares. Every other button in the ladder — 25 cents, 30 cells, and onward up to 60 cents — is sized the same way: divide the fixed dollar risk amount by that button's specific stop-loss distance in cents to get the share count for that button.

Second is the order type for entry, which is set to a stop-limit order, and both the stop price and limit price are defined relative to wherever the trader clicks on the chart — described in the platform as "chart trader price" — rather than as fixed absolute dollar figures, since the whole point of a hot button is that it works at whatever price level the trader happens to click on any given day. The course's specific configuration sets the stop price at one cent above the clicked chart price and the limit price at five cents above the clicked chart price, meaning the order becomes active the instant price trades one cent above the click point, and it is permitted to fill anywhere up to five cents above that click point before the limit constrains it from chasing further.

Third and fourth are the bracket's exit legs, both also defined relative to the clicked chart price rather than as fixed dollar amounts. The stop loss is configured as a stop order set at the button's own labeled distance below the chart trade price — 15 cents below for the "Buy 15 cents" button, 20 cents below for the "Buy 20 cents" button, and so on. The profit target is configured as a limit order set at exactly double that same distance above the chart trade price — 30 cents above for the "Buy 15 cents" button (risking $0.15 to make $0.30, a ratio the course states outright as risking one dollar to make two, or a fixed 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio), and 40 cents above for the "Buy 20 cents" button. Every button in the ladder follows this same fixed doubling rule: whatever the stop distance is, the target distance is exactly twice it.

Once these four settings are entered for a given button, the course flags one additional toggle worth understanding clearly: the "Transmit order instantaneously" (or "arm") setting. When a button is armed, clicking it on a live chart submits the entire bracket order immediately and irrevocably the instant the click registers — there is no confirmation step. The platform displays an explicit warning to this effect, and the course tells the trader to read that warning and only proceed once it is understood, since an armed button truly does fire the position, stop, and target the moment it is clicked. If a trader prefers a manual check before every entry, the arm setting can be left unchecked, in which case clicking the hot button populates the order onto the chart with everything pre-filled but requires a separate, deliberate click on a "Transmit" control to actually send it. Once the desired settings and arm state are chosen, clicking "Apply" saves the button, and it then appears permanently in the chart's button panel, ready to be reused on any future trading day; the whole ladder — Buy 15 cents through Buy 60 cents — is built by repeating this exact same process once per stop-loss distance the trader wants available. The full button panel can be hidden or shown at will via the chart's View menu.

In live use, once a stock is loaded onto the Guns Trade tab and the trader has identified where they want to buy — say, at the high of a specific candle — the workflow is to pick the hot button matching the desired stop-loss distance (a stop the trader has decided is appropriate based on the setup, for instance choosing the "Buy 20 cents" button because 20 cents is judged the right distance for that particular candle) and then click directly on the chart at the exact price level to be used as the entry point, which in the course's example was the high of the chosen candle. The platform immediately draws three horizontal lines on the chart representing the pending entry, the stop loss, and the profit target at their calculated distances, and the corresponding bracket order appears in the order panel showing the exact number of shares that will be bought. If the market is genuinely live and price subsequently trades up through the entry line, the order fires automatically with no further action required (when armed); if the market is closed, as in the course's own demonstration, the order simply sits unfilled. At any point after a position is open, the trader retains full manual control: the stop loss can be raised (for example, moved up to lock in a partial gain as price moves favorably) by clicking on the order and entering a new stop price, then confirming with "Amend" or "Update," which immediately redraws the stop line on the chart at its new level; an unfilled pending order can be canceled outright with a "Cancel" or "Delete" control; and an already-open position can be flattened instantly at the prevailing market price at any time by clicking the platform's "Close Position" button — a control the course specifically flags as useful for exiting early if the trade looks like it is about to reverse even before the stop or target has been reached.

### Preparing the Watchlist: The Daily Pre-Market Routine

With the trading station built, the second half of this lesson turns to the ritual that happens before any of it gets used on a given day: assembling the watchlist. The course's instructor, trading from Singapore, begins this ritual at 9:00 a.m. Singapore time, which corresponds to the pre-market hours before the US market's 9:30 a.m. Eastern open, and the entire watchlist-building process is compressed into a fixed twenty-five-minute window, running from that 9:00 a.m. start through 9:25 a.m. Eastern. The goal at the end of that window is a short list of only four to six stocks — narrowed down, deliberately, from what may be as many as ten candidates that technically qualify — because the instructor is explicit that human attention cannot meaningfully track more than four to six setups at once, and trying to watch ten stocks simultaneously produces worse decisions than focusing narrowly on the best few.

#### Step One: Source the Candidate List

The first source used to find gapping stocks is a free website, stockmarketwatch.com, specifically its pre-markets section. Navigating to Premarkets on that site surfaces a live table of stocks that are gapping up against the previous session's closing price, along with each one's percentage change and its pre-market share volume. The second source, built and run in parallel, is the custom Mosaic Market Scanner configured directly inside the Guns Trade tab of the trading station itself, using the exact criteria detailed above. A third source is mentioned as an optional paid alternative for traders who want a more powerful, customizable screener: a subscription service called Trade Ideas, priced by the course at around $100 per month or roughly $1,000 per year, which allows a trader to build a fully custom scan for gapping stocks in the same spirit as the free tools. The instructor's own stated personal habit is to lean primarily on the free stockmarketwatch.com site and the self-built Interactive Brokers scanner, treating Trade Ideas as something other traders may choose to use but not something relied on personally.

#### Step Two: Apply the Hard Filters

Whichever source or combination of sources is used, every candidate stock is run through the same set of hard filters before it is allowed onto the watchlist, and the course states each threshold explicitly:

| Filter | Required value |
|---|---|
| Last price | At least $1.50 |
| Pre-market volume | At least 30,000 shares traded |
| Percentage gap | At least 5% above the previous session's closing price |
| Instrument type | Common stock of an actual company (exchange-traded funds are explicitly excluded) |

Any stock that fails any one of these thresholds is dropped from consideration immediately, regardless of how compelling its news story might otherwise be. The course walks through a worked example using the stockmarketwatch.com table on a specific sample day: one stock (referred to in the transcript as "Sino") is gapping up 23% and clears the volume threshold, so it stays; a second stock is up 15% and also clears volume, so it stays; a third stock clears the 5% gap threshold but its pre-market volume is judged too low, so it is discarded outright; several more candidates are likewise discarded purely for insufficient volume; another candidate, referred to as NTNX, clears both the percentage and volume thresholds and stays; and at least one further candidate that would otherwise qualify is discarded anyway because it turns out to be an exchange-traded fund rather than an individual company's stock, and ETFs are excluded from the watchlist on principle. Whatever remains after this filtering pass is then ranked from highest pre-market volume to lowest, on the reasoning that higher volume generally signals a more liquid, more actively-traded, and more reliably tradeable setup.

#### Step Three: Read the News Catalyst

Every stock that survives the hard filters must then be checked for the specific nature of its news catalyst, because not every piece of news that moves a stock is equally tradeable. The course lists the catalyst types it actively favors: an earnings report that beat analyst expectations (described as the instructor's own favorite type of catalyst); FDA approval of a drug or medical product, which signals to the market that the company is now cleared to generate sales and profit from that product; an analyst upgrade, in which a covering analyst raises a stock's rating — for example from "buy" to "strong buy" — and simultaneously raises the target price, both of which tend to draw fresh buying interest; and a clinical trial passing successfully, which for a pharmaceutical or biotech company represents one more hurdle cleared on the path to eventual drug approval and tends to build fresh momentum with every successful trial result.

Just as important as the list of catalysts to favor is the specific catalyst type the course says to actively avoid: a stock being acquired by another company. The reasoning given is entirely mechanical rather than a matter of taste. If a company's stock is trading at $5 and it is announced that another company is acquiring it for $7 per share, the market will very quickly reprice the target company's stock straight up toward that $7 acquisition price and then largely stop moving, because the acquisition price effectively sets a ceiling — there is little reason for the market to bid the stock meaningfully above the agreed buyout price. A day trading strategy built around continued momentum after an initial pop has nothing to work with in that situation, since the very news that caused the initial pop is also the reason the stock is unlikely to keep running afterward; the course's instruction here is simply not to trade stocks whose gap is driven by acquisition news, regardless of how large the initial percentage move looks.

Two places are named for actually finding and reading the specific news behind a candidate stock. Inside Interactive Brokers itself, clicking on any stock symbol automatically surfaces its recent news directly inside the Guns News tab built earlier in this chapter, which is exactly why that tab was given a dedicated news panel in the first place. Outside the broker platform, the free website FinViz — reached at finviz.com — displays a company's most recent news headlines at the bottom of its individual stock page once a ticker symbol is entered into the site's search box; Think or Swim offers a comparable live news feed of its own, reached through a "Live News" control inside that platform. The course's own worked example of reading a catalyst through this process involves a company referred to as Luxo (ticker LOXO), whose stock gapped up pre-market on news that its experimental tumor drug had produced a 76% objective response rate in trials — a result interpreted by the market as strong evidence the drug works well enough that it is likely to eventually gain approval and help treat patients, which is precisely the kind of unambiguous, fundamentals-driven catalyst the strategy is built to trade.

#### Step Four: Check the Float

Beyond price, volume, and catalyst quality, the course adds one more structural filter: the size of the stock's free float. A publicly traded company has a total number of shares outstanding, representing every share the company has ever issued, but not every one of those shares is actually available to be bought and sold day to day. Some portion may be held privately and unavailable for sale — the course's illustrative example describes a company with 100 million shares outstanding of which 50 million are held by a founder who refuses to sell under any circumstances, leaving only the remaining 50 million shares as the free float, meaning the pool of shares genuinely available for public trading, which represents the effective supply side of the stock's market.

The course's reasoning for why float size matters to a day trader is a straightforward supply-and-demand argument. When free float is small, the available supply of shares is small, so it takes relatively little fresh buying demand for demand to exceed supply, and when demand exceeds supply for a scarce good, price tends to move sharply and quickly. Conversely, when free float is large, it takes a correspondingly large amount of fresh demand to meaningfully move the price at all, because there is simply more supply available to absorb that demand without much price impact. For a strategy that depends on rapid, forceful price movement inside a matter of minutes, small float stocks are therefore structurally more attractive candidates than large float stocks. The course's specific numeric guidance: any stock with a free float under 100 million shares is considered low float and worth favoring, and stocks with a float in the 10 to 20 million share range are considered very low float and are described as the ideal candidates — the course notes this is exactly the kind of stock capable of moving from, say, two dollars to five dollars in a single day, a 400% gain, purely because there are so few shares available that even modest new demand overwhelms the available supply. Free float figures for any given ticker are found on the same FinViz page used to check news — once a ticker is entered on finviz.com, the front page displays a field labeled "Float" (or "Free Float") on the right-hand side of the page alongside the stock's other key statistics.

#### Step Five: Evaluate the Daily Chart

Every stock still on the shortlist after the float check is then examined on its daily chart, checked against the same four moving averages configured across the trading station — the 9 EMA, 20 EMA, 50 SMA, and 200 SMA. The ideal daily chart shows the stock already in a clear uptrend, trading above all four moving averages with no major overhead resistance level standing between the current price and further upside, since any such resistance level sitting between the entry price and the trader's profit target risks stopping the move before the target is ever reached.

The course is explicit, however, that a stock does not have to already be in an established daily uptrend to qualify — a stock that was previously in a clear daily downtrend, trading below its moving averages, remains tradeable if the specific news catalyst is strong enough to gap the price up and above those same moving averages in one move, because once price clears above the moving averages, the moving averages that used to act as a ceiling holding price down instead flip into acting as a potential floor supporting it, leaving what the course calls "clear skies ahead" with nothing structurally standing in the way of further gains. The course's worked contrast is a stock referred to as GLYC, whose daily chart shows a clear downtrend into the news event, but whose gap, driven by the catalyst, jumps the price up cleanly above all of its moving averages in a single move — a chart the course says is entirely fine to trade despite the prior downtrend, precisely because of where the gap lands relative to those moving average levels.

Even a stock that gaps up only partway through its moving-average stack — clearing, say, the 50-day moving average while remaining below the 200-day moving average — can still be tradeable, as long as there is enough clear distance, or "window," between the current price and the next moving-average resistance level to accommodate the trade's own profit target. Since a GUNS scalp typically only needs to capture something on the order of a 30-cent stop paired with a 60-cent target, a stock does not need a completely clear runway all the way to its all-time highs; it only needs enough clear room to plausibly reach that modest target before running into the next resistance level. Beyond the moving averages themselves, the course adds one more preference on the daily chart: ideally the stock is found pulling back slightly after a strong initial impulse move, resting near rather than far above its moving averages, because a stock that has already run very far above its own moving averages is considered "overextended" and has correspondingly less room left to run further, whereas a stock resting closer to its moving averages after an initial pop still has substantial room to run once buying resumes.

#### Step Six: Evaluate the Five-Minute Pre-Market Chart

The final and, in the course's own framing, most important piece of chart analysis is the five-minute pre-market chart, examined on the same platform (the course uses Think or Swim for this specific illustration purely because its charts are considered visually clearer, though the analysis applies equally on Interactive Brokers' own charts). The grey-shaded region of this chart represents the pre-market session itself, the trading that takes place before the official 9:30 a.m. open, and it is this grey region the trader studies most carefully in the final minutes before the bell.

Several specific conditions define what the course considers an ideal pre-market chart, and each is stated as a concrete, checkable criterion rather than a vague impression:

1. Price must be trading above all four moving averages on the pre-market chart — the 9 EMA, the 20 EMA, the 50 SMA, and the 200 SMA — with no averages sitting above price acting as overhead resistance.
2. Price should be consolidating close to the pre-market high, which is defined as the single highest point reached at any point during the pre-market session; the course draws a horizontal resistance line directly at this level on every candidate chart, describing it functionally as a gate that price must clear before it can be expected to run further once the regular session opens. The ideal distance between the current consolidating price and that pre-market high is within about 5% of the high; the outer acceptable limit the course gives is about 20% below the high, beyond which the setup is considered too far from its own resistance level to be attractive.
3. The stock should not be overextended relative to its own recent pre-market move — the course does not attach a specific numeric threshold to this criterion, framing it instead as a qualitative judgment developed through experience of comparing many charts side by side.
4. The stock should ideally not have already run for an extended period before the current morning's pre-market session — meaning that if the underlying news was actually released the previous evening, after the prior day's regular session had already closed, the stock may have already spent many hours moving in the after-hours and overnight session before the current pre-market window even begins. A pre-market chart with many candles already printed, showing a long history of overnight movement before the current pre-market grey region even starts, signals — in the course's own metaphor — that "the party has already begun long ago" and that the trader arriving at the open is arriving late, with most of the easy move already captured by earlier participants. A pre-market chart with comparatively few candles, showing the move as fresh and just getting underway, is markedly preferred, since it implies more of the move is still ahead rather than already behind.

The course illustrates these criteria with several worked comparisons. Among charts it holds up as good examples: a stock referred to as Coda that ran up to a pre-market high of $9.48, then consolidated within roughly 10 to 20% of that high, was judged acceptable; a stock referred to as GEN that made a pre-market high of $1.92 and was consolidating between its 9 and 20 EMAs, still above both, was judged acceptable; and a third example that made a pre-market high of $5.45 and pulled back to find support directly at its 9 EMA while remaining close to the high was also judged acceptable, alongside a fourth example with a pre-market high of $1.65 that had retraced somewhat further but was still holding above its moving averages.

Among charts the course holds up as poor examples, and explains why each fails: a stock referred to as IRBT showed a pre-market chart with a large number of candles already printed from the previous evening, indicating the move had begun hours before the current pre-market window and that a trader entering fresh that morning would already be late, and its price had also sold off back down below its 50, 20, and 9 moving averages, turning those averages into resistance rather than support; a stock referred to as NVRO was trading below its 200 moving average, again converting that average into overhead resistance, and its chart additionally showed a large number of gaps between candles, meaning price was jumping between levels without much continuous trading in between — a sign, the course notes, of thin liquidity that makes for a less reliable setup; a third chart described only as "generally okay" nonetheless had a similar problem of gaps and incomplete candles suggesting insufficient continuous liquidity even though its price action relative to the pre-market high and moving averages looked otherwise reasonable; and a fourth chart showed extensive candle history built up well before the current pre-market session even started, again the "late to the party" problem, combined with price trading below its moving averages on a clear downtrend.

The course is careful to note what a poor pre-market chart does and does not rule out. A stock whose pre-market chart fails these criteria is not placed on the list of stocks eligible for an order entered before the market opens, but it is not necessarily discarded from the watchlist altogether — the stock can still be watched once the regular session opens, in case the live opening price action reshapes itself into a genuinely tradeable pattern once real volume and real participants arrive, at which point it might still qualify for one of the later setups that are only ever taken after the opening bell has already rung, rather than one of the setups placed in advance based purely on pre-market structure.

#### Step Seven: Drawing Resistance and Choosing a Setup

Once a stock has cleared every filter above, the last preparatory task before 9:25 a.m. is to mark up its charts with every relevant resistance level the trader can identify — most importantly a horizontal line drawn exactly at the pre-market high, since that level is the first real obstacle price must clear once trading resumes, and additional horizontal lines drawn at whichever moving averages sit above the current price on the daily chart, since each of those represents a further potential resistance level the price will have to fight through as the day develops. With those levels marked, the trader decides, purely from the shape of the pre-market chart, which of the strategy's five specific entry setups — referred to simply as Setup One, Setup Two, Setup Three, Setup Four, and Setup Five, and reserved for full explanation in the strategy lessons that follow this chapter — the stock's current price action actually supports. Setups One, Two, and Three are, notably, setups whose triggering order can be placed and armed before the market even opens, based entirely on the shape of the pre-market chart already visible; Setups Four and Five, by contrast, can only be identified and taken once the regular session has actually opened and live one-minute candles begin printing in real time.

If, by 9:25 a.m., the trader has identified one or more stocks whose pre-market structure clearly supports a Setup One, Two, or Three entry, the corresponding order is built on a hot button and armed in advance, ready to fire the instant the market opens and price clears the marked resistance level. If no stock on the watchlist supports any of those first three setups, no order is placed in advance at all — the trader instead does nothing until the bell rings, then watches for a Setup Four or Setup Five pattern to develop live on the one-minute chart once real trading volume begins. In either case, in the final moments before 9:30, every five-minute chart still being watched on the Guns Charts M5 tab is switched over to one-minute resolution, giving a much more granular view of exactly how price behaves candle by candle as the opening bell approaches and then rings — at which point, in the instructor's own words, the trader takes a breath, the market opens, and the watch for a live setup begins in earnest, carrying the entire routine built across this chapter directly into the strategy lessons that follow.

# Part II — The Strategy Setup, Reading Level 2 Quotes & Live Trading Examples

## The Strategy Setup: Five Parts, Reading Level 2 Quotes, and Live Trading Examples

The moment finally arrives in the GUNS course where the instructor, Adam Koo, sets aside the groundwork of trading-station configuration and watchlist preparation and turns to the thing every student has been waiting for: the actual rules by which a trade is entered, protected, and exited. He is explicit that this is the most consequential part of the entire curriculum, because everything that came before it — the platform setup, the scanner configuration, the pre-market gap screening — exists only to feed candidates into this decision engine. There are, he says, exactly five valid setups in the GUNS strategy, no more and no fewer, and they split cleanly into two families defined by timing rather than by chart pattern. Setups One, Two, and Three are pre-market setups: the orders for these are constructed and transmitted to the broker before the opening bell, while the trader is still looking at the five-minute pre-market candle chart, so that the order is already resting in the book the instant the market opens and the price either runs into it or it does not. Setups Four and Five, by contrast, can only be taken after the opening bell, because they are built entirely from the one-minute (or, less often, two-minute or five-minute) candles that begin to print the moment regular trading hours start. This chapter walks through all five in the order they were taught, narrates the case study in which the instructor builds a live watchlist and fires off pre-market orders in real time, explains how to read the Level 2 order book that sits behind every one of these decisions, and finally narrates a full live trading session end to end, tying every action back to the rules established here.

A note on how this chapter treats the source numbers is worth stating up front, because the instructor himself is not always perfectly precise when speaking live over a moving chart, and this account preserves that imprecision rather than smoothing it away. On more than one occasion he rounds a price, corrects himself mid-sentence, or gives two slightly different figures for the same level a few seconds apart as he re-measures a candle or a pivot on the fly. Where the transcript shows this kind of live self-correction, the corrected, final figure is the one used here, and where a screen value is referenced only vaguely — a candle described as "about" a certain size, or a pre-market high estimated rather than read precisely off a drawn line — that same vagueness is preserved rather than replaced with an invented precise figure. No number appearing anywhere in this chapter has been estimated, rounded, or supplied where the source material did not actually state it.

Before getting into the individual setups, it is worth naming the two ideas that recur underneath every one of them, because once a student internalizes these two ideas the five setups stop looking like five unrelated patterns and start looking like five variations on a single argument. The first idea is that the pre-market high is the single most important price level on the chart for the first hour of trading. It is the first, most obvious overhead resistance the stock has to fight through, because every trader who was watching that stock in the pre-market session saw the same high print and is now watching to see whether the market-open crowd can push through it. Because of this, the pre-market high behaves like a gate: price runs up to it, gets rejected, and sits underneath it "smelling it," in the instructor's words, the way a person stands at a starting gate waiting for it to open. Every one of the five setups either enters just as price is about to test that gate, enters below it with a plan to exit before hitting it, or enters immediately after the open in the belief that the crowd will punch through it on the very first attempt. The second idea, which threads through every setup without exception, is the 1R risk unit and its disciplined use for both position sizing and trade management. The instructor defines 1R as the exact dollar distance between the entry price and the stop-loss price — in other words, the amount of money at risk per share if the trade goes wrong. Every profit target in the course is stated as a multiple of this same 1R, almost always 2R or 2.5R, so that a trader who loses is capped at losing 1R while a trader who wins captures two to two and a half times that amount. And every trade management rule in the course reduces, in the end, to a single instruction: the moment a trade moves 1R in your favor, you move your stop loss up to your own entry price, converting a trade that could still lose money into a trade that, at worst, breaks even. This is the mechanism that lets a trader with a fifty percent win rate — one winner, one loser, on average, every single day — still come out ahead over time, because the loser costs exactly 1R while the winner returns something between 1.5R and 2.5R.

It is also worth naming, before working through the individual setups, exactly how the instructor thinks about position sizing, since the same $100-per-trade framework recurs throughout every worked example in this chapter regardless of which of the five setups is being taken. Because the stop-loss distance changes from trade to trade — ten cents on a cheap stock, a dollar or more on an expensive one — the number of shares purchased has to change inversely to keep the dollar risk constant. A trader who decides to risk $100 per trade and is using a ten-cent stop can afford to buy 1,000 shares, because 1,000 shares times a ten-cent adverse move equals exactly $100 of loss if the stop is hit; the same trader using a thirty-cent stop can only afford roughly 333 shares for the identical $100 of risk; and using a full dollar stop on an expensive name, the share count drops to roughly 100. This inverse relationship between stop distance and position size is why the instructor is comfortable using such wildly different absolute stop distances from one trade to the next — ten cents on one stock, a dollar-thirty on another — without ever describing the strategy as inconsistent: the dollar risk taken on the account is held constant across every trade, and it is the share count, not the stop-loss cents figure, that does the adjusting. This is also the reason high-priced stocks create the specific cash-versus-margin problem discussed under Setup One, because even a modest share count multiplied by a high per-share price can require far more capital than a modest account actually holds, forcing the trader either toward CFDs, toward margin, or away from the stock altogether.

### Setup One: Entering the Break of the Pre-Market High

Setup One is, by the instructor's own account, the setup he takes more often than any other besides Setup Four, and it is the natural place to begin because it is built directly on the pre-market-high concept just described. The condition for taking Setup One is narrow and specific: the pre-market price must be consolidating very close to the pre-market high, ideally less than five percent below it, so that the stock is, in his image, a runner standing right at the gate rather than a runner still a hundred meters back down the track. When a trader pulls up the five-minute pre-market chart and sees the candles climbing to a high and then flattening out just beneath that high rather than sliding back down away from it, that is the visual signature of a Setup One candidate.

The mechanics of the entry are worked out to the cent. The order is a buy-stop-limit order, placed at or just before 9:28 or 9:29 a.m. Eastern time, one minute or two before the opening bell, so there is no need to rush; a trader can take a full sixty to ninety seconds to enter the order carefully. The stop price of the buy order sits exactly one cent above the pre-market high — always one cent, regardless of the stock's absolute price — so that the order is only triggered once price has genuinely broken through the level rather than merely touched it. Attached to that stop price is a limit price set three to five cents above the stop price; this limit exists purely as a ceiling on how much the trader is willing to overpay if the stock gaps hard through the entry the instant the bell rings, and the instructor is candid that on a fast-moving name the order can simply fail to fill if price rockets past the limit before the broker can execute it — a possibility a trader must accept rather than chase.

The stop-loss that protects the trade is described first as a rule of thumb tied to the stock's own price, and then refined into a more precise, data-driven method. As a rule of thumb: for a stock trading under twenty dollars, the stop distance is roughly ten to fifteen cents; for a twenty-to-thirty-dollar stock, twenty to thirty cents; for a thirty-to-fifty-dollar stock, thirty to fifty cents; and for anything above fifty dollars, roughly fifty cents. But the instructor is emphatic that the more accurate approach — and the one he actually uses when precision matters — is to flip the chart from the five-minute timeframe used to spot the setup over to the one-minute candle timeframe and read off the Average True Range, the ATR, of those one-minute bars. If the one-minute ATR reads twenty cents, the stop distance for that trade is set at twenty cents; this single number becomes the trade's 1R. The profit target is then placed at roughly 2R to 2.5R beyond the entry price — so if the risk per share is twenty cents, the target sits forty to fifty cents above the entry.

Once the order is transmitted at 9:29, the trader's job is to flip every chart over to the one-minute candle timeframe and simply watch for the bell. Three distinct outcomes are described, and each one carries its own instruction. In the ideal outcome, the market opens and price runs immediately up through the entry price, filling the buy order right away and continuing to run; the moment unrealized profit reaches 1R, the trader moves the stop loss up to the entry price, locking in a guaranteed break-even floor before the trade goes any further, and then simply lets the position run toward the 2R–2.5R target. In the second outcome, the market opens and price sells off immediately, moving away from the entry rather than toward it; here the instructor's rule is to wait roughly three to five minutes to see whether the stock turns around, and if it still looks weak after that window, to cancel the order outright and redirect attention toward a Setup Four candidate instead, because a stock that opens weak and stays weak for five minutes is unlikely to reward patience. In the third outcome, price does move up toward the entry but moves so violently fast that it blows straight through the limit price without ever filling the order; here the rule is equally blunt — do not chase the stock by moving the order up to catch it, simply cancel and move on, because chasing a runaway breakout defeats the entire purpose of having a pre-calculated risk-to-reward ratio.

The instructor then works through five concrete case studies to cement the pattern in the student's mind, and each one is worth walking through because the numbers illustrate exactly how tight and mechanical the execution really is. On TGI, the five-minute pre-market chart showed the stock climbing to a pre-market high of $29.00 and then consolidating right at that level — a textbook setup. The buy-stop was placed at $29.01, one cent above the high, with a limit five cents higher at $29.06. The stop loss was set twenty cents below entry at $28.81, making 1R exactly twenty cents, and the profit target was placed fifty cents above entry — 2.5R — at roughly $29.51. When the market opened, the very first one-minute candle triggered the buy at $29.01, and within the second one-minute candle price had already touched $30.00, comfortably clearing the $29.51 target for a clean 2.5R winner in under two minutes.

On CLSN, a much lower-priced stock at roughly $3.96, the same architecture was applied at a smaller scale appropriate to the stock's price. The pre-market high sat at $3.96, and because this was a low-priced stock the buy-stop was set at $3.97 with only a three-cent limit buffer at $4.00, rather than the five-cent buffer used on higher-priced names — a reminder that the buffer, like the stop distance, should scale with the price of the stock rather than being applied as a fixed constant. The stop loss was placed ten cents below entry at $3.87, making 1R ten cents, and the profit target was set at 2.5R, twenty-five cents away, at $4.22. The market opened, price ran immediately, the buy order filled at $3.97, and the $4.22 target was struck within the very first one-minute candle.

It is worth pausing on why the instructor insists on placing the buy-stop exactly one cent above the pre-market high rather than exactly at it. If the order were placed at the same price as the high itself, it would fill the instant price merely retested the old level without necessarily confirming that the level had been overcome — and a retest that fails, stalling right at the old high and rolling back over, is a fundamentally different piece of price action from a genuine breakout that closes above the level with conviction. The extra cent is a deliberately small but non-negotiable buffer that forces the market to prove the level has actually been taken out before capital is committed. This same logic, scaled up or down according to the price of the instrument, recurs in every one of the five setups: Setup Two's one cent above the pivot, Setup Three and Setup Four's one cent above the pullback candle's high, and Setup Five's one cent above the first candle's high are all the same underlying idea — never buy the level itself, always buy confirmation that the level has been cleared.

The limit-price buffer plays a related but distinct role. Because the entry order is a stop order that converts to a live market order the instant the stop price is touched, there is, in principle, no cap on how much a trader might end up paying if the stock is moving extremely fast at the moment of the fill — a five-cent stop could, in theory, fill fifty cents higher on an illiquid, fast-moving name if no limit were attached. The three-to-five-cent limit exists purely to cap that downside without preventing the order from filling under normal, orderly conditions. The trade-off the instructor accepts explicitly is that on the rare occasion where a stock truly explodes through the level in a single tick, the order may simply not fill at all, and he is unambiguous that the correct response to a missed fill of that kind is to let it go rather than chase the price upward by moving the limit — chasing defeats the entire purpose of pre-calculating a fixed risk-to-reward ratio before the trade is even placed.

On FIV, a considerably higher-priced stock near $97.94, the instructor pauses the setup narration to address a structural issue that arises specifically with expensive stocks: position sizing on a strategy that uses such tight stop-loss distances (ten, twenty, fifty cents) can force a trader to buy far more shares than their account can comfortably hold in cash, sometimes more shares than their entire account balance would allow to purchase outright. His prescribed solution is to trade high-priced stocks — he uses fifty dollars and above as a rough threshold — using CFDs (contracts for difference) rather than buying the underlying shares outright, because a CFD position requires only a fraction of the notional value in cash margin; a twenty-thousand-dollar CFD position, in his example, might only tie up two thousand dollars of actual cash, roughly ten percent, versus the full twenty thousand a cash purchase of shares would require. Absent access to CFDs, his alternative is to use margin borrowed from the broker, and if a trader is uncomfortable with either mechanism, his advice is simply not to trade high-priced stocks with this strategy at all. Returning to FIV itself: the pre-market high was $97.94, the buy-stop was set at $97.95, and because it is a high-priced stock the limit buffer was widened back out to five cents, to $98.00. The stop loss was set at a thirty-cent distance, making 1R thirty cents, with the profit target placed at roughly 2R, around sixty cents above entry. The trade triggered at the open, ran up, and struck the target — though the instructor notes it was a genuinely close call, because price reversed almost immediately after tagging the target, meaning a target set even slightly higher would have missed the exit window entirely and turned a winner into a round trip back to breakeven or worse.

On ASTC, a low-priced and easy-to-trade stock, the pre-market high sat at $6.94. The buy-stop was placed at $6.95, with a three-cent limit buffer to $6.98, and the stop loss set at a ten-cent distance to $6.85, again making 1R ten cents. The profit target was set at 2.5R, twenty-five cents above entry, at $7.20. The market opened, price ran immediately through the entry, and the target was struck — again inside the very first one-minute candle.

Across every one of these five worked examples the same skeleton repeats: mark the pre-market high, place the buy-stop one cent above it, set a limit buffer of three to five cents scaled to the stock's price, size the stop loss either by the price-tiered rule of thumb or, more precisely, by the one-minute ATR, and set the profit target at 2R to 2.5R beyond entry. What varies from trade to trade is only the absolute dollar figures — the pattern itself never varies.

Two failure modes are also described for Setup One, and both matter as much as the winning examples because they define when a trader should walk away rather than force a trade. In the first failure mode, the market opens and price simply never approaches the entry price at all — it opens below the pre-market high and sells off further rather than climbing toward it. The instructor's rule here is to give the stock roughly five minutes to show any sign of turning back up; if five minutes pass with no such sign, the order is cancelled outright, and the trader's attention pivots entirely away from Setup One and toward hunting for a Setup Four opportunity on the very same stock, since a stock that fails to run at the open can still produce a valid post-open bull flag later in the same session. In the second failure mode, price does move toward and even through the entry level, but it moves so explosively fast that the order's limit price is left behind and the fill never happens; here too the instruction is simply to cancel rather than to chase the price upward by adjusting the limit, because doing so would mean buying an already-extended move with no discipline behind the entry price at all. A third, more favorable variant is also described: the ideal case where price runs immediately at the open, fills the order right away, and continues running without hesitation — and the instructor notes that in his own experience, this immediate, hesitation-free acceleration is characteristic of his best-performing Setup One trades, while a fill that comes only after a slow, grinding climb is statistically more likely to stall out before reaching the full profit target.

### Setup Two: Entering the Break of the Pre-Market Pivot

Setup Two exists to handle the case where price made a pre-market high some time ago but has since pulled back and is now consolidating well below that high rather than right up against it — the situation Setup One is specifically unsuited for. The instructor's reasoning here is important to internalize, because it explains why a trader would ever choose to enter below the pre-market high rather than waiting for the break of the high itself: if price still has to travel a meaningful distance up to the pre-market high before hitting resistance, buying that lower level captures the move without buying at the top of the immediate range where the risk of an abrupt reversal is highest. Buying directly at the pre-market high after it has already run some distance risks entering just as the move exhausts itself.

The pattern that defines Setup Two is a price sequence in which the stock rallies to a pre-market high, sells off away from that high, and then forms a second, lower consolidation point — a pivot — some distance beneath the original high. Once price is sitting near that pivot rather than near the original high, the trader draws a horizontal line at the pivot's own local high and places a buy-stop one cent above that pivot line, with the same three-to-five-cent limit buffer used throughout the strategy. The stop loss again follows the price-tiered rule of thumb or, more precisely, the one-minute ATR, sitting ten to fifty cents below the entry depending on the stock's price. The profit target is the familiar 2R to 2.5R above entry — but there is a critical additional constraint layered on top of Setup Two that does not appear in Setup One: the pivot must sit far enough below the pre-market high that the trade can realistically capture at least a full 1R of profit before running into the pre-market high itself as resistance. If the pivot is too close to the pre-market high, there simply is not enough room between the entry and the next resistance level to make the trade worthwhile, and the instructor's instruction in that case is not to take Setup Two at all. The reasoning ties directly back to the trade-management rule already established: once a trade reaches 1R of unrealized profit, the stop is moved to breakeven, so a Setup Two entry needs at minimum a full 1R of clean room between the pivot and the pre-market high in order for that critical stop-adjustment to ever have the chance to happen before the pre-market-high resistance is tested.

The worked example is WB, a considerably high-priced stock. On the five-minute pre-market chart, price rallied above the moving averages to a pre-market high, sold off but stayed above the moving averages throughout the pullback — a detail the instructor stresses matters, because a pullback that breaks the moving averages undermines confidence that the broader uptrend is intact — and then carved out a pivot at $70.89. The buy-stop was placed one cent above that pivot at $70.90, with a limit buffer of five to seven cents. The stop loss was set at $70.60, a distance of roughly thirty-five cents, making 1R thirty-five cents, and the profit target was placed at least 2R away, roughly seventy cents above entry, at $71.65. When the market opened, the first one-minute candle triggered the buy at $70.90 and, within that same single one-minute candle, price ran all the way to roughly $72.80 — far beyond the $71.65 target — closing out the trade for a clean win well inside the first minute of the trading day.

The WB example is also useful for illustrating why Setup Two exists as a genuinely distinct decision from Setup One rather than merely a fallback for stocks that happen to be a little further from their pre-market high. Had a trader instead waited for WB to reach its actual pre-market high before entering, the entry price would have been considerably higher, and the distance available to run before that same pre-market high acted as resistance would have collapsed to nothing, because price cannot run further past a level it has already reached. By entering instead at the pivot, well below the pre-market high, the trader buys the stock while there is still genuine room between the entry and the next resistance level, converting what would otherwise be a resistance test into tradable distance. This is why the rule requiring the pivot to sit at least a full 1R away from the pre-market high is treated as non-negotiable rather than a nicety: without that separation, a Setup Two entry degenerates into buying directly beneath a wall with no room to profit before the wall is reached.

### Setup Three: Entering the New High of the Pre-Market Bull Flag

Setup Three requires a longer conceptual detour, because before the setup itself can be defined the instructor first has to define, in careful and repeated detail, exactly what a bull flag is — a definition that will resurface unchanged when Setup Four is introduced, since Setup Four is simply the same bull-flag pattern applied after the opening bell rather than during the pre-market session. A bull flag begins with a run of impulse candles: a burst of buying that pushes price up in a series of candles, each one making a higher high than the one before it. The instructor is careful to strip away several assumptions students might bring to this idea. The impulse candles do not all need to be green or bullish-bodied candles — what matters is only that each successive candle's high exceeds the prior candle's high, regardless of whether the candle itself closed up or down. There is likewise no fixed minimum number of impulse candles; a single, sufficiently forceful bullish candle can constitute the entire flagpole on its own, provided it represents genuinely strong buying pressure in the direction of the trend.

After this burst of buying, some profit-taking inevitably follows, producing a pullback — one or more candles that make lower highs than the high set by the impulse move. A pullback candle may take the specific shape of an inside bar, meaning its entire range (both high and low) sits inside the range of the prior candle, or it may simply print a lower high alongside a lower low; either shape qualifies as a valid pullback candle for the purposes of the pattern. Visually, the impulse candles form the pole of the flag and the pullback candles form the flag itself, hence the name. There is no fixed minimum number of pullback candles either — one is sufficient, though two or three commonly appear — but there is one hard structural requirement that the instructor repeats insistently: the pullback candles must hold above the stock's short-term moving averages, ideally finding support at the 9-period or 20-period exponential moving average, or at worst the 50-period moving average. The moment a pullback candle's low breaks decisively beneath those moving averages, the pattern is invalidated, because at that point the pullback has stopped looking like routine profit-taking within an intact uptrend and started looking like a trend reversal.

Applied specifically to the pre-market session, Setup Three requires that this entire sequence — impulse candles building a flagpole, followed by pullback candles building a flag, with the flag holding above the moving averages — be visible on the five-minute pre-market chart, with the final pullback candle still printing a lower high right up until the moment the market opens. The entry is a buy-stop-limit order placed one cent above the high of that final pullback candle, with the usual three-to-five-cent limit buffer. The stop loss sits just below the low of that same final pullback candle — this becomes the trade's 1R distance, which should still respect the ten-to-fifty-cent guideline (or the one-minute ATR, for precision) appropriate to the stock's price. The profit target is, once again, 2R to 2.5R above entry. And exactly as with Setup Two, the pre-market high still functions as an overhead resistance level that the trade needs enough room to clear before running into it, so if the final pullback candle sits too close to the pre-market high already, the instructor's guidance is to fall back to Setup One instead of forcing a Setup Three entry into a pattern with no breathing room. He is candid that judging when a pullback is "too close" to the pre-market high to justify Setup Three versus Setup One is a matter of accumulated screen-time experience rather than a bright numerical line, and he notes bluntly that in his own trading, roughly ninety-nine percent of his pre-market trades end up being Setup One rather than Setup Two or Setup Three — those two remain in the toolkit for the specific chart shapes that call for them, but they are the exception rather than the rule.

Two worked examples close out the pre-market half of the strategy. On MTBC, the five-minute pre-market chart showed three impulse candles stepping up to successively higher highs, forming the pre-market high, followed by two pullback candles each printing a lower high while resting on the 9-period moving average. The buy-stop was placed one cent above the high of the final pullback candle, the stop loss placed just below that candle's low to define 1R, and the profit target set at 2R (or better, if 2R could be reached before the pre-market high) beyond entry; the trade was then simply monitored on the one-minute chart after the open to see the target or stop struck. On KERX, an identical structure appeared: a run of higher highs into a pre-market high of $23.95, followed by a sequence of pullback candles finding support at the 20-period moving average, with the final pullback candle serving as the trigger candle for the same buy-stop-above-high, stop-below-low, target-two-R-away construction. Both examples are presented as clean, representative bull-flag setups rather than as unusual or marginal cases, reinforcing that the pattern — impulse, pullback holding the moving average, entry on the final pullback's high — is meant to be recognized quickly and mechanically once a student has seen it enough times.

It is worth dwelling on why the moving-average condition is treated as an absolute disqualifier rather than as one factor among several. The entire logic of a bull flag rests on the idea that the pullback candles represent temporary, healthy profit-taking within a still-intact uptrend rather than the beginning of a genuine reversal. The short-term moving averages — the 9-period and 20-period exponential averages in particular — function as the trader's proxy for "is the uptrend still intact," because they represent the average price paid by recent buyers over those respective lookback windows. As long as the pullback candles hold above those averages, the working assumption is that the buyers who have been driving the stock higher over the last several bars are still, on balance, sitting on unrealized profits and therefore have every incentive to defend the level rather than sell out of it. The moment a pullback candle's low punches through those averages, that assumption breaks down — some of the recent buyers are now underwater and have every incentive to sell to reduce their loss, which is precisely the dynamic that turns an orderly pullback into a disorderly, self-reinforcing decline. This is also why the instructor is willing to tolerate real variation in every other dimension of the pattern — one impulse candle or five, one pullback candle or three, bullish-bodied pullback candles or bearish-bodied ones — while treating the moving-average support condition as the one dimension that is never negotiable.

The same logic explains why the instructor repeatedly emphasizes that judging the boundary between a valid Setup Three and a better-suited Setup One takes accumulated screen time rather than a fixed numerical rule. A pullback candle sitting only a few cents beneath the pre-market high technically satisfies every structural requirement of a bull flag, but entering there via Setup Three's mechanics — buy-stop above the pullback high, stop below the pullback low — would leave essentially no room to reach even a single 1R of profit before the pre-market high intervened as resistance, which is exactly the scenario the room-to-run requirement is designed to prevent. In that specific circumstance the instructor's guidance is to recognize that the situation has effectively become a Setup One in disguise — the trade to take is the break of the pre-market high itself, not the break of the final pullback candle sitting just beneath it — which is part of why he estimates that, in his own trading, the overwhelming majority of pre-market entries end up being classified as Setup One even when the underlying chart technically also displays bull-flag characteristics.

### The Case Study: Building the Pre-Market Watchlist and Firing the Orders

Having taught the theory of the three pre-market setups, the course then shows the entire process assembled end to end in a single case study, recorded at 9:07 a.m. Eastern time — roughly twenty-three minutes before the opening bell — which makes it possible to see exactly how much (or how little) time a trader actually has to do this work every single morning.

The instructor begins at stockmarketwatch.com, on its pre-market gap-up scanner page, screening specifically for stocks gapping up more than five percent with at least a five-figure (that is, tens of thousands of shares) volume figure. At the moment he checks, only three names clear that bar: VRTX, SNI, and XGTI. He notes explicitly that this list is not final — between 9:07 and 9:30, new names can and often do appear on the gap scanner as more pre-market volume accumulates, so the watchlist has to stay live and be rechecked repeatedly right up until the bell.

For each of these three candidates he runs the same three-step qualification sequence. First, the daily chart, to check whether the pre-market gap is running the stock straight into a wall of prior daily resistance. VRTX was gapping up toward $167.25 with no overhead resistance in the way — "clear skies ahead," in his words. SNI was gapping toward $78 with no resistance in sight. XGTI was gapping toward $2.30, sitting just above its 200-day moving average, which he treats as acceptable rather than as a red flag. Second, the news, checked on Finviz, to identify the specific catalyst driving each gap — because a gap with no identifiable news behind it is a much weaker candidate than one with a clear story attached. VRTX had genuinely powerful news: shares had jumped 24% to an all-time high after positive cystic fibrosis drug trial data, precisely the kind of binary, high-conviction catalyst the instructor flags as capable of sustaining a strong move; its average daily volume was checked at 1.7 million shares against a float of 247 million shares. SNI's news was a pending merger between Discovery Communications and Scripps Networks — merger talk rather than a completed deal, but still, in his assessment, a legitimate catalyst capable of sending a stock to new highs; its volume and float were checked at roughly 1.4 million and 3 million respectively. XGTI's news was more mundane — the company had received a $1.5 million equipment order for microwave communications hardware — but he treats a concrete business order as a sufficient catalyst nonetheless, especially paired with a very small float of only about 9 million shares against 1.2 million shares of volume, a combination he specifically likes because a small float against real volume tends to produce outsized, fast price moves. Third, having cleared both the resistance check and the news check, he moves each surviving name onto his "Guns Grid" five-minute pre-market chart template to look for the actual entry pattern.

On the five-minute chart, VRTX had already run up considerably in the pre-market — its high sat at $173, well above the then-current price of roughly $166 — which the instructor flags as a stock a trader is "a bit late to the party" on; rather than chasing the pre-market high directly (Setup One), he opts instead to look for a Setup Two entry at a pivot he measures at approximately $168.83, placing his intended entry one cent above that, at $168.84. On SNI, price was sitting right near its own pre-market high of $78, so this qualifies cleanly as a Setup One, with an intended entry at $78.02, one cent above the high. On XGTI, the chart showed a stock that had already made a high near $2.50 and then carved out a sequence of successively lower pivots; the instructor selects the most recent of these pivots, at $2.40, for a Setup Two entry one cent above it at $2.41.

With ten minutes left before the bell, a fourth name, ALSK, appears freshly on the gap scanner. He checks it quickly and finds its pre-market volume sitting at only 141,000 shares — below the roughly 200,000-share threshold he treats as his minimum for taking a name seriously — and discards it on liquidity grounds alone, without going any further into its chart or news, illustrating how quickly a marginal candidate gets cut once it fails a single hard filter.

He then moves to actually placing the three surviving orders with his broker, Interactive Brokers, walking through the position-sizing logic explicitly as he goes. For a stock trading around $160 like VRTX, he uses roughly a thirty-cent stop loss; framing the whole exercise around a maximum risk of $100 per trade (his stated recommendation for a trader just starting out), a thirty-cent stop translates into a position size of 333 shares. The order itself is entered as a buy-stop-limit at $168.84 with a five-cent limit buffer to $168.89, and a bracket order is attached carrying a stop loss thirty cents below entry at $168.54 and a profit target set at 2.5R — seventy-five cents above entry — at $169.59. For SNI, trading near $70, the same thirty-cent stop is used, again sized to keep the dollar risk near $100, giving an entry at $78.02, a limit at $78.07, a stop loss thirty cents below entry at $77.72, and a 2.5R target seventy-five cents above entry at $78.77. For XGTI, a roughly two-dollar stock, a much tighter ten-cent stop is used, sized to 1,000 shares to keep the dollar risk at the same $100 ceiling; the entry is set at $2.41 with a three-cent limit to $2.44, a stop loss ten cents below entry at $2.31, and a 2.5R target twenty-five cents above entry at $2.66.

With the orders in and only a few minutes left before the open, the instructor keeps re-checking the pre-market tape, because prices keep drifting even in these final minutes. He notes VRTX is trading below its own moving average and looks unlikely to trigger, but leaves the order resting anyway rather than cancelling early. He notices SNI's price creeping up toward his entry level even before the bell — at one point the pre-market high itself shifts up to $78.30 — forcing him to shift his own resting order upward in real time, from $78.02 to $78.35, to stay one cent above the new, higher pre-market high rather than leaving a stale order sitting beneath a level that has already been broken.

It is worth noting how much of this entire process — the gap scan, the two-stage daily-chart and news check on three separate candidates, the five-minute chart annotation of each one's pre-market high and any pivots, the ATR read on the one-minute chart, and finally the position-sizing arithmetic and order entry on three separate names — is compressed into a window of only about twenty-three minutes, from 9:07 to 9:30. The instructor does not present this as leisurely research; he presents it as a drilled, repeatable sequence executed under a hard deadline every single morning, which is itself part of why the earlier lessons on configuring hot buttons and templated chart layouts matter so much — without that preparation, the same analytical process could not physically be completed for even a single stock in the time available, let alone three or four.

The results, once the bell rings and he switches every chart to the one-minute timeframe, play out exactly according to the rules already taught. SNI triggers almost immediately: the stock gaps through the shifted entry, fills the buy order, and runs straight to the profit target within the very first one-minute candle, exiting for roughly a seventy-cent-per-share gain, which on the position size used works out to about $200 of profit in well under sixty seconds of actual market time. VRTX, by contrast, opens and immediately drops away from the entry level rather than running toward it; watching the one-minute chart confirm the weakness, the instructor cancels the order rather than waiting for it to trigger. XGTI likewise opens to the downside; he notes he does not wait the full five minutes on this one because the early one-minute price action already looks unpromising, and cancels that order as well after roughly four minutes of no movement toward the trigger level.

The session's arithmetic is stated plainly at the close of the case study: three orders were placed, two failed to trigger and were cancelled with zero cost, and one triggered and captured a clean win — SNI, bought at $78.35 and sold at $79.05, a seventy-cent-per-share gain across 333 shares for $233 of profit against $100 of risk, a better-than-2R outcome. The instructor uses this single day's arithmetic to make a broader point about the compounding potential of the strategy: risking $100 per trade and averaging roughly $200 of profit per trading day, across twenty trading days in a month, works out to roughly $4,000 a month — a figure he immediately qualifies by urging students to start with small risk amounts like $100 while they build consistency, rather than scaling position size before the process itself is proven out.

The case study also demonstrates, in a way the standalone setup explanations cannot, how much active management the pre-market entries require even before the market opens. SNI's pre-market high itself moved while the order was already resting in the book, rising from $78.02 to $78.30 in the final minutes before 9:30, and the instructor's response was not to leave the stale order sitting where it was but to shift it upward in real time to $78.35, staying one cent above the newly updated high. This detail matters because it shows that "placing the order at 9:29" is not a single, static action but the beginning of a continuously monitored process that can require adjustment right up until the opening bell itself. A trader who places an order at 9:29 and then looks away until 9:30 risks leaving an order sitting beneath a pre-market high that has since moved higher, which would convert what should be a confirmed breakout entry into a premature entry below a level that has not, in fact, been broken yet.

### Setup Four: Entering the First Bull Flag After the Open

With the three pre-market setups established, the course turns to the two setups that can only be traded after the opening bell, beginning with Setup Four, which the instructor identifies, alongside Setup One, as one of the two setups that make up the overwhelming majority of his actual trading activity. The pattern itself is the identical bull flag already defined for Setup Three — the same impulse-candles-into-a-flagpole followed by pullback-candles-into-a-flag structure, with the same requirement that the pullback candles hold above the 9- or 20-period moving average — but the critical distinction is that for Setup Four, the entire pattern must form on the live one-minute (or occasionally two-minute or five-minute) candles printing after 9:30 a.m., rather than on the pre-market five-minute chart. Setup Four is described as typically appearing within the first hour of trading, and most often within the very first five to thirty minutes after the open.

The mechanics of spotting and entering the pattern in real time are described with unusual granularity, because this is the setup that most requires split-second execution using pre-configured hot buttons rather than a manually typed order ticket. The trader watches the one-minute candles print one at a time as the market opens, waiting to see a run of impulse candles making successively higher highs. The moment a candle closes having made a lower high than the prior candle — the first pullback candle, or inside bar — the trader is now permitted to place an entry order: a buy-stop one cent above the high of that pullback candle, with a stop loss placed one cent below its low. If a further candle closes and it, too, makes only another lower high rather than breaking above the pullback candle just used for the order, the existing order is cancelled and a fresh order is placed one cent above the high of this newer, lower pullback candle instead — the entry point keeps sliding downward, candle by candle, following the flag as it forms, until either a new high is finally made (triggering the order) or the pattern breaks down entirely by falling beneath the moving averages (invalidating the setup outright).

Before pulling the trigger on any Setup Four entry, the instructor lists two checks that must be run in the moment, and both echo directly into the section on Level 2 quotes covered later in this chapter. First, the bid-ask spread on the Level 2 screen must be checked and must be tight — under five cents ideally, and never wider than five cents for this setup specifically, because he states plainly that anything wider is "full of risk" and he will simply decline the trade regardless of how clean the chart pattern looks. Second, the trader must scan for any obvious nearby resistance — whether from the pre-market high, from a moving average on the daily chart, or from a visibly large sell order sitting on the Level 2 ask just above the intended entry — because a large resting sell order at a nearby price level represents a wall of committed sellers that the trade would have to fight through immediately after entry, and the instructor states he will avoid the trade entirely if he sees one there.

The position sizing and order-placement workflow for Setup Four is built entirely around hot buttons pre-configured on the trading platform, because there simply is not time to type a full order ticket between candle closes. As soon as a valid pullback candle closes, the trader eyeballs its height in cents — is it a ten-cent candle, a twenty-five-cent candle, a forty-cent candle — and clicks the correspondingly pre-configured hot button (buy with a fifteen-cent stop, buy with a twenty-five-cent stop, and so on), which simultaneously fires a buy-stop order at whatever price the trader's cursor is hovering over on the chart, with the matching stop-loss and 2R profit target already attached automatically. This single click-and-hover motion replaces manual price entry entirely, and it is this that lets the entire sequence — measuring the candle, selecting the risk level, placing the stop, placing the target — happen inside the handful of seconds available between one candle closing and the next one opening.

This is also the point in the course where the earlier lesson on configuring the trading station stops being background setup and becomes the operational backbone of the strategy itself. A hot button, once configured, encodes three decisions simultaneously: the stop-loss distance in cents, the profit target as a fixed multiple of that same distance, and the share count needed to keep the dollar risk of the trade constant regardless of which stop distance is selected. Because a trader typically pre-builds a small bank of these buttons at several common stop distances — fifteen cents, twenty cents, twenty-five cents, forty cents, and so on — the only decision left to make in the heat of the moment is which of the pre-built buttons most closely matches the height of the candle just measured, and where to hover the cursor. Everything else — the stop level, the target level, the position size — has already been decided in advance, during the calm of pre-market preparation, precisely so that none of it has to be decided under the time pressure of a live, one-minute candle closing in real time. Without this infrastructure, Setup Four and Setup Five would simply not be executable at the speed the strategy requires; the entire value of the hot-button system is that it moves every slow decision to before the market opens and leaves only the fast, purely visual decision — how tall is this candle, and does it clear the moving average — to be made live.

Several supplementary rules govern Setup Four specifically. The instructor states a strong preference for entering only on the very first bull flag that forms after the open, rather than the second or third, on the grounds that later flags occur after price has already run considerably further, making the entry inherently riskier even though he acknowledges some traders he knows successfully trade the second and third flags as well. He offers a numerical guardrail for how deep a pullback is allowed to be relative to the impulse move that preceded it: the pullback should retrace no more than fifty to sixty percent of the impulsive wave's total move; a retracement all the way back toward the full one hundred percent of the impulse is treated as evidence the uptrend has lost its strength rather than as a valid flag to buy. Framed differently, if the impulsive wave up is imagined as spanning from 0% at its starting point to 100% at its high, a valid flag pulls back only partway down that same span — to roughly the 40%-to-50% retracement level, leaving the majority of the original move still intact — rather than round-tripping all the way back to where the impulse began. A pullback that erases the entire prior advance is, in the instructor's framing, no longer a pause within a strong trend; it is evidence that the trend itself has failed, and the correct response is to stand aside rather than to buy the presumed continuation. Spread discipline is repeated with a specific threshold for this setup: a spread wider than ten cents makes him reluctant to enter (an exception being made for very high-priced stocks where a wider absolute spread is proportionally normal), and he separately avoids any stock whose minimum tick size is a nickel rather than a penny, because a five-cent minimum tick removes the fine-grained control over entry and stop pricing the strategy depends on. Finally, he restates the resistance-clearance rule that has now appeared in every setup: the entry must sit far enough below the pre-market high, or any other overhead resistance level, that the trade can realistically capture a full 1R of profit before running into that resistance.

Trade management for Setup Four repeats the now-familiar 1R stop-to-breakeven rule, but adds one further, optional layer specific to this setup: the instructor notes that in addition to shifting the stop to breakeven once 1R of profit is banked, or once the trade approaches a known resistance level like the pre-market high, a trader watching the Level 2 quotes in real time can also choose to exit early — before either the stop or the target is struck — the instant a large sell order appears on the ask just above the current price, because that large resting order signals a seller is about to try to push price back down through the position. He is careful to describe this as optional rather than mandatory: some traders, he notes, simply place the order with its stop and target already attached and then walk away entirely, a "fire and forget" approach that works perfectly well; watching the Level 2 tape for an early exit is an enhancement available to traders who want to actively manage the position rather than a requirement of the strategy itself.

The instructor then works through a long run of concrete Setup Four examples, each one reinforcing the identical mechanical sequence with different numbers. In the first example, the market opens with five successive one-minute candles each making a higher high — a clean five-candle flagpole — followed by two inside-bar pullback candles whose highs are effectively equal to each other, so no order is placed on the first of the two (since it did not make a genuinely lower high relative to the one before it), and only once the second inside bar prints a clear lower high does the trader act. He measures that candle's height at roughly ten cents, clicks the fifteen-cent hot button (accounting for a slightly wider buffer than the raw candle height to absorb the spread), places the cursor at the candle's high, and the resulting buy-stop-limit fills at $5.98 with a stop at $5.73 and a target at $6.61; the very next candle triggers the fill, and the position reaches its 2R target roughly three and a half minutes after the open.

On a second, unrelated example on ASTC, the instructor notes that this particular stock's pre-market chart had actually looked poor and would not have made his pre-market watchlist at all, but it re-emerged as a Setup Four candidate purely from his live market-open scanner once regular trading began — a reminder that the pre-market watchlist and the live, post-open scanner are two separate and complementary sources of candidates, and a stock can qualify for Setup Four even having failed every pre-market filter. On this name, the first one-minute candle opened as a strong bullish bar closing above the moving averages, the second candle extended the impulse to a new high, and the third candle pulled back to a lower high while finding support directly on the moving average — a textbook single-pullback bull flag. The pullback candle measured roughly forty cents in height; the trader clicked the forty-cent hot button, placed the order at the candle's high, and the very next candle triggered the fill, reaching its 2R target (an 80-cent move on a 1-to-2 risk-reward ratio) within the following one-minute bar.

On OPHD, a stock that had shown a decent but not compelling pre-market chart — not clean enough for Setup One, Two, or Three, in the instructor's assessment — the live open produced three impulse candles into a flagpole followed by a first pullback candle. An order was placed above that first pullback's high, but the next candle made only another lower high rather than triggering it, so the order was cancelled and replaced one cent above the new, lower pullback candle, which was again confirmed to be resting on the moving average for support. That second pullback candle measured roughly fifteen cents; the fifteen-cent hot button was used, the next candle triggered the fill, and the position reached its 2R target within three minutes. The instructor notes in passing that had a 2.5R target been used instead of 2R on this particular trade, it likely would have taken longer to fill — a small aside illustrating the trade-off between target size and holding time that a trader implicitly accepts with every choice of 2R versus 2.5R.

On SDOT, the same three-impulse-candle flagpole formed, followed by a single inside-bar pullback; the entry was placed with a twenty-cent stop and a forty-cent target, filled on the next candle, but this particular trade took a genuinely long time to resolve — roughly ten to twenty minutes by the instructor's own count as he narrates it — which he uses to make the observation that even inside a strategy built around one-minute candles, a single trade can occasionally stretch out to what feels, in the compressed emotional time of active trading, like "an eternity."

On SCVL, the pattern again produced a first pullback candle whose order was never triggered because the following candle made a still-lower high and low, requiring the order to be cancelled and replaced beneath the newer pullback candle; that replacement order did trigger on the next bar, and the 2R target was struck within a single one-minute candle — the fastest of the examples given. The instructor notes wryly that after exiting for the full 2R, the stock continued running further still, and his stated attitude toward that outcome is simply to take the planned profit without regret, because "you never know how far it's going to go," and chasing an already-closed winning trade for additional upside undermines the discipline the entire risk-defined structure is built on.

On CPAH, the flagpole extended unusually long — five successive impulse candles making higher highs before the first pullback candle finally appeared — illustrating that the flagpole length is genuinely variable and a trader should not become impatient or assume a pattern has failed simply because the impulse phase runs longer than in other examples. The first pullback order was again cancelled once the next candle made a still-lower high and low, the order was replaced beneath the new pullback candle with a twenty-cent stop and forty-cent target, and the subsequent candle triggered the fill; the position closed out for its 2R target roughly six minutes later.

Two further examples extend Setup Four beyond the one-minute timeframe to demonstrate that the pattern is genuinely scale-invariant. On an Apple-related instrument traded on the two-minute chart — used, the instructor explains, by traders who find the one-minute timeframe too fast or stressful to watch continuously — four impulse candles built the flagpole, and the entry order slid downward across two successive failed pullback attempts before finally triggering on a pullback candle sitting very close to the moving average; the resulting trade ran cleanly to its target. On VRAY, traded on the five-minute chart when no clean one-minute setup was visible that day, a single large impulse candle constituted the entire flagpole on its own — reinforcing the earlier point that one big candle is sufficient — followed by a single inside-bar pullback measured at forty cents; the forty-cent hot button was used, the target was set at double that distance (an 80-cent, 2R move), and the position closed out for its full target within three five-minute candles, or fifteen minutes of chart time. The instructor notes that watching a five-minute and a one-minute chart side by side, simultaneously, on the actual trading desk is standard practice, so that a valid five-minute pattern is never missed simply because attention is fixed on the faster timeframe.

Taken together, these seven Setup Four examples are also a lesson in how frequently the very first pullback order gets cancelled and replaced before it ever fills. In at least four of the seven cases described, the initial order placed above the first pullback candle's high was never triggered, because the very next candle printed a still-lower high, forcing the trader to cancel and re-place the order one cent above the newer, lower candle instead — sometimes more than once in succession. The instructor treats this repeated cancel-and-replace behavior as entirely normal and expected rather than as a sign that the setup is failing; the trigger only matters once a candle actually closes above the most recent pullback high, and until that happens, the order simply tracks the pullback downward, candle by candle, exactly as a trailing marker would. What ends the tracking is either a valid trigger — a new high finally made — or a break of the moving averages, at which point the entire setup is abandoned rather than chased further downward. This is also why hot buttons are indispensable to Setup Four specifically: manually re-typing a full order ticket every time a pullback candle closes without triggering would consume far more of the available one-minute window than the strategy allows, whereas a single click-and-hover motion lets the order be relocated in the handful of seconds between one candle's close and the next one's open.

### Setup Five: The Simplest Setup — the First Bullish Candle

Setup Five is described as the simplest of all five setups in the entire strategy, and it applies exclusively to the one-minute candle timeframe — unlike Setup Four, it cannot be adapted to the two-minute or five-minute chart. The entire condition for taking Setup Five is that the very first one-minute candle printed after the 9:30 a.m. opening bell closes as a bullish candle above all of the moving averages, and that this candle is not unusually large relative to the size of the candles that printed during the pre-market session — in fact, the instructor states explicitly that the smaller this first candle is, the better the setup, since a huge first candle suggests the easy move has already happened and chasing it invites buying at an already-extended price.

The execution is correspondingly simple: the moment that first bullish one-minute candle closes, the trader places a buy-stop one cent above its high, with the usual limit buffer, a stop loss placed just below the low of that same candle (defining 1R), and a profit target of 2R to 2.5R above entry. Exactly as with every other setup, the entry must be checked for spread — the same five-cent-ideal, ten-cent-maximum guidance applies — and for whether there is sufficient room to reach at least 1R of profit before running into the pre-market high or any other overhead resistance. And exactly as with Setup Four, because the entire sequence has to be completed inside the single minute between the first candle's close and the next candle's open, this setup depends entirely on pre-configured hot buttons rather than manual order entry.

Two worked examples are given. On OPHD — the same stock that later produced a Setup Four entry, illustrating how a single name can generate more than one valid setup across a session — the first one-minute candle after the open was bullish and closed above the moving averages; its height was measured at twenty cents, the twenty-cent hot button was used, the buy-stop-limit and its attached twenty-cent stop and forty-cent target were placed automatically at the candle's high, and the entry filled on the very next candle. The position took roughly fifteen minutes to reach its target. On ATTU, the first one-minute candle was again bullish, closed above the moving averages, and was not unusually large; its height was measured at thirty cents, the thirty-cent hot button was used, and the resulting order filled at $14.21 with a stop at $13.95 (spelled out as 30 cents away) and a target at $14.81 — a 60-cent move on the risked thirty cents, a clean 2R trade. The entry triggered on the next candle and the target was struck within four one-minute candles, or roughly four minutes of trading.

The instructor closes the theoretical portion of Setup Five, and the five-setup framework as a whole, with a final anecdote that ties every setup together into a single coherent workflow: on July 16th, none of the names on his pre-market watchlist produced a valid setup of any kind, and by the open he had concluded there would be no trade that day. Then, at 9:32 a.m., a name called CALI appeared on his live scanner — a two-dollar stock gapping up 27% on unusually heavy volume, not a name that had appeared on his pre-market list at all. Because it had already printed several one-minute candles by the time he noticed it, Setup Five (which requires acting on literally the first candle) was no longer available, but a valid first-pullback Setup Four pattern was still forming, so he took that instead: the pullback candle measured twenty cents, the twenty-five-cent hot button was used to buy at $2.75 with a stop at $2.50, the following candle triggered the fill, and the position — after a long, roughly hour-long hold during which price stalled sideways before eventually grinding up to the fifty-cent, 2R target — closed out as a winner. The anecdote is deliberately included to demonstrate that a trader's watchlist is a starting point rather than an exhaustive universe: the live scanner must be watched continuously through the entire session, because a stock with no pre-market footprint at all can still produce a perfectly valid Setup Four an hour after the open, and the discipline is to keep the same rules — measure the pullback, size the hot button, place the stop, place the target — running regardless of where the candidate originally came from.

Setup Five is also worth contrasting directly against Setup Four, because the two setups are frequently confused by students on first exposure despite being structurally distinct. Setup Four requires waiting for an entire flagpole-and-flag sequence to form — at least one impulse candle followed by at least one pullback candle — before an entry can even be considered, and the entry itself comes on the break of the pullback candle's high rather than on the very first candle of the session. Setup Five, by contrast, requires no flagpole and no pullback at all; it is a bet placed on the very first candle of the day in isolation, on the theory that a small, controlled bullish candle right out of the gate is itself the signal, rather than being merely the first leg of a larger pattern that still needs to develop. This is precisely why Setup Five cannot be adapted to the two-minute or five-minute timeframe the way Setup Four can: the entire premise depends on catching literally the first candle printed after the bell, and on any timeframe slower than one minute, by the time that first candle closes, several minutes of actual trading — and, typically, a meaningful move in price — have already elapsed, which defeats the purpose of entering before the crowd. The CALI anecdote makes this distinction concrete: because several one-minute candles had already printed by the time the instructor noticed the stock, the window for Setup Five had already closed, and the only setup still available to him on that same chart was Setup Four's first-pullback entry, arriving later and by definition at a less advantageous point in the move.

### Reading Level 2 Quotes: The Order Book Behind Every Decision

Having established all five setups, the course turns to a tool that sits underneath every single one of them without being a setup itself: Level 2 quotes, also called market depth. The instructor is careful to frame this correctly from the outset — Level 2 reading is useful only for intraday trading of exactly the kind the GUNS strategy practices, it is not something a swing trader needs to concern themselves with, and it is not, in his own words, an absolute requirement for being successful with this strategy. A trader can be profitable without ever learning to read Level 2. What it provides instead is an edge on top of an already-sound setup: a way to filter out trades that look clean on the chart but are structurally dangerous underneath, and a way to exit a position early, before either the stop loss or the profit target is struck, when the order book itself starts signalling danger.

The Level 2 screen displays two columns side by side for any given stock. On the right sits the ask column, representing every seller currently queued up in the market, each one posting, in the instructor's phrase, a "for sale" sign at a chosen price. The ask column is arranged with the lowest, most competitive ask sitting at the top of the list and the highest, least competitive asks sitting toward the bottom — sellers willing to accept a lower price get priority and rise to the top of the queue, while a seller demanding an unreasonably high price simply sits at the back. On the left sits the bid column, representing every buyer currently queued up, each one offering to buy at a chosen price; here the ordering runs the opposite direction, with the highest, most aggressive bid sitting at the top (because a buyer willing to pay the most gets served first) and progressively lower, less competitive bids stacked beneath it. Every quantity shown on either side is denominated in round lots of 100 shares, so a figure of "1" on the screen represents 100 shares and a figure of "21" represents 2,100 shares.

Level 1 data — the single best bid and single best ask — is the starting point, and the difference between that best bid and best ask is called the spread. The instructor states plainly that a stock's spread must be as tight as possible to be tradable at all under this strategy: a spread under five cents is ideal, and he personally will not trade a stock carrying a spread wider than roughly ten cents, because the act of entering and exiting the position effectively forces the trader to cross that spread twice, eating directly into the very tight profit margins the strategy's 1R/2R structure depends on.

Level 2 depth extends this picture by showing every subsequent bid and ask stacked behind the best price on each side, and this is where the strategic reading actually happens. When a buy order is being placed, the instructor identifies three distinct order types available, corresponding to three distinct places the order can be routed. Placing a market buy order fills immediately at whatever the current best ask is — the trader is, in effect, agreeing to pay whatever the most competitive seller is currently asking. Placing a limit buy order down on the bid side, by contrast, means joining the queue of existing buyers at a chosen bid price and waiting for a seller to come down and meet that price rather than paying up to meet the seller. And a buy order can also be placed anywhere between the best bid and best ask, effectively offering to split the spread with whichever side moves first. For entries under this strategy specifically, the instructor states his own practice is almost always to buy at the market — because a buy-stop order that triggers converts immediately into a market order once the stop price is touched, filling at whatever the ask happens to be at that instant, with the previously discussed limit price attached purely as an upper ceiling to prevent an unacceptably bad fill if price is moving unusually fast. The same three-way choice exists on the exit side, mirrored: selling at the market fills immediately against the best bid (useful for getting out of a losing trade quickly, since the trader wants speed rather than an extra cent or two of price), while selling on the ask side means joining the queue of sellers and waiting for a buyer to come up and meet that price, which the instructor reserves for situations where the position is already comfortably in profit and there is no urgency to exit immediately.

Beyond the mechanics of order placement, the instructor lays out four specific things he checks on the Level 2 screen before ever clicking a buy button, and these four checks function as a final filter layered on top of every setup already covered. First, the bid-ask spread itself must be tight, repeating the five-cent-ideal, ten-cent-ceiling rule already stated. Second, he wants to see prices between consecutive bid levels sitting close together — bids stacked at every one-cent interval going down from the current price, rather than large gaps between one bid level and the next — because tightly packed bids indicate deep, continuous demand at every nearby price point. Third, and closely related, he wants to see those individual bid levels carrying meaningful size — genuinely large resting orders, in the thousands of shares, rather than thin two-hundred-share bids — because a large resting bid functions as a support level: it represents real committed capital that will absorb selling pressure and hold the price up, whereas a screen showing a small 200-share bid at one level and then no further bids until a price several cents lower is, in his words, "very dangerous," because the moment that thin 200-share bid is exhausted there is genuinely nothing standing between the current price and a much lower price, and a stop loss set in that gap could get run over instantly. Fourth, and working the same logic in the opposite direction on the sell side, he wants to confirm there is no large resting ask order sitting just above his intended entry price, because a large ask functions as resistance in exactly the way a large bid functions as support: it represents a seller committed to dumping a large block of shares at that level, and if price reaches that level before the trader's own profit target, the wall of supply can stall or reverse the move before the target is ever reached.

He offers a specific numerical illustration of this last point using a hypothetical stock trading near $12.08: a trader intending to buy at the market (at the ask, $12.08) notices that at $12.09 — just one cent above the intended entry — there is a resting sell order for 14,000 shares. That single order, he explains, functions as a hard ceiling; if price ticks up to $12.09 and that seller begins actually executing against incoming buy orders, there may not be enough buying pressure left to push through the wall, and the trade stalls right at the entry with no room to move favorably. His stated response to seeing a wall like that is simply not to enter — unless he watches that large resting size actually shrink in real time, tick by tick, as buyers absorb it (his example has it shrinking from 14,000 down through 140 and then 150 lots, which reads as the size dwindling as it's eaten into), which he interprets as evidence that the sellers are being overwhelmed by genuine buying demand rather than holding the level; only once he sees that active absorption happening does he consider the wall to be neutralized and the entry safe to take.

The mirror-image use of Level 2 applies to managing a position that is already open and already profitable. If the trader is in a long position working toward, say, a $15.00 profit target, and a large resting sell order suddenly appears on the ask at $14.90 — just short of the target — the instructor's stated response is to exit the position immediately, closing it out for whatever profit is available at that moment rather than waiting to see whether the large seller actually gets through, absorbed, or holds the line. He restates this principle explicitly at the close of the lesson: exit a trade early whenever a large ask appears above the current entry and before the target has been reached, and likewise exit or tighten the stop whenever the bid side thins out unexpectedly beneath the current price. The one qualifier he adds is that a large ask is not automatically disqualifying if it is being paired with equally large, absorbing bid size beneath it — a large seller who is being actively eaten by large buyers is a very different signal from a large seller sitting alone above thin support, and distinguishing between those two pictures on the live tape is, in the end, the entire skill this section of the course is teaching.

It is worth being precise about what Level 2 reading is not being used for in this strategy, because the temptation for a new trader is to treat the order book as a predictive tool in its own right — something that can generate its own trade ideas independent of the chart. The instructor is explicit that this is not how it functions here. Every Level 2 check described in this lesson is a filter applied on top of a setup that has already been identified on the candlestick chart using the rules from the preceding five sections; Level 2 never originates a trade by itself. Its two jobs are narrowly defined: first, to veto an otherwise valid chart setup when the order book underneath it looks structurally unsound — too wide a spread, too thin a bid, too large a resting ask directly overhead — and second, to trigger an early exit on an already-open position when the book's structure deteriorates before the stop loss or profit target is reached. A trader who tries to use Level 2 as a standalone signal generator, scanning the order book for interesting-looking size without a chart pattern already in hand, is using the tool for something it was never built to do in this course.

There is also a practical reason the instructor gives for why this filter matters more for the strategy taught here than it would for, say, a swing trader holding a position for days or weeks: the stop-loss distances used throughout GUNS are extremely tight, often just ten, twenty, or thirty cents, which means the trade has essentially no room to absorb an adverse structural surprise in the order book. A swing trader with a stop several dollars away can shrug off a temporary imbalance in the bid-ask structure because the position has enough room to weather it; a GUNS trader with a twenty-cent stop cannot, because a single thin patch in the bid column sitting between the entry and the stop can be the entire difference between the stop holding and the stop being blown through instantly on the first wave of selling. This is the underlying reason the instructor treats a wide spread or thin depth as disqualifying regardless of how attractive the chart pattern looks on its own — the strategy's entire edge depends on risk being genuinely capped at the stated 1R, and an order book that cannot support that containment undermines the strategy's core assumption before the trade has even begun.

### Live Trading Examples: A Full Session Narrated End to End

The final lesson of this stretch of the course is a complete, unedited walk-through of one actual trading day, beginning with the pre-market watchlist build and ending with a debrief of every trade taken, and it functions as a live demonstration of every rule established across the preceding sections operating together in real time.

The instructor begins his routine, as always, just before 9:00 a.m. Eastern time — roughly 9:00 p.m. Singapore time, since he trades U.S. markets from Singapore — and works through stockmarketwatch.com's pre-market gap scanner looking for stocks gapping up at least 5%, priced above $1.50, and carrying at least 30,000 shares of pre-market volume. His initial pass turns up IHT, NSYS, and Tesla. He cross-checks this against his own scanner inside the trading platform, which sometimes surfaces names the website misses and vice versa — a deliberate redundancy, he notes, so that nothing slips through. The cross-check turns up ETP (showing over a million shares of pre-market volume already), along with BCRX, BIOC, DXCM, and ESIO, though he screens BCRX out immediately for sitting below the 150,000-share volume floor he wants to see at that stage, and passes over several of the others for carrying comparatively lower relative volume than his preferred top four to six names. He consolidates the survivors — IHT, NSYS, Tesla, ETP, BCRX, BIOC, DXCM, and ESIO — onto his platform watchlist and begins working through news and daily-chart resistance for each one in turn, exactly as demonstrated in the earlier case study.

Tesla's catalyst was earnings — a quarterly loss that nonetheless beat revenue estimates, a classic "less bad than feared" earnings reaction — with a clean daily chart showing no overhead moving-average resistance, and a five-minute pre-market chart sitting very near its own pre-market high, which he marks at $335.76, flagging it immediately as a strong potential Setup One. DXCM had also just reported earnings, likewise gapping up clear of daily resistance, and its five-minute chart showed consolidation near a pre-market high he marks at $118.50 (rounding to $118.59 in his own annotation), another Setup One candidate. ESIO had also just reported earnings — a 26-cent-per-share beat alongside a strong first-quarter result — gapping above daily resistance, with its five-minute chart sitting relatively near a pre-market high he marks at $21.50, a third Setup One candidate, though he notes he would have preferred the price to be sitting even closer to that high than it was. ETP's catalyst was merger news, and while its daily chart looked clean, its five-minute pre-market chart showed price sitting too far below its own pre-market high — which he marks at $23.25 — to qualify as a Setup One; he earmarks it instead purely as a possible Setup Four or Five candidate once the market actually opens. IHT's news was comparatively weak — the company was simply selling off a property asset for a modest profit — and while its daily chart was clean, its five-minute chart showed price too far from its own pre-market high (marked around $2.70) to support a pre-market entry; like ETP, it is set aside for a possible post-open setup rather than discarded outright. BIOC had no identifiable news and sat beneath moving-average resistance on the daily chart, so he loses interest in it immediately. BCRX's catalyst turned out to be a new-share offering — a dilutive event, though its five-minute chart showed price sitting right at a pre-market high near $5.80, keeping it alive as a borderline Setup One candidate even as its gap percentage was drifting down toward the 5% floor. NSYS, up roughly 10% and clean on the daily chart, was trading well beneath its own moving averages on the five-minute pre-market chart, ruling out Setup One or Two entirely and leaving it, at best, a speculative Setup Four or Five candidate once the bell rings.

Two further names surface as the clock runs down: QTRH, whose five-minute chart shows price sitting nowhere near its own pre-market high (marked at $1.76) despite clearing daily-chart resistance, is set aside for a possible post-open setup only; and W, whose five-minute chart looks considerably more promising, with a pre-market high marked at $117.00 and price consolidating close enough beneath it to flag as a genuine Setup One candidate. For every one of these live candidates, the instructor repeats the same stop-loss-sizing method taught throughout the earlier lessons: flip to the one-minute pre-market candles and read the ATR. For Tesla, the one-minute ATR reads out at roughly two dollars, so a two-dollar stop is planned if Tesla is traded. For W, the one-minute ATR reads roughly $1.33, and he rounds that up to a $1.20–1.30 planned stop distance.

Two further names, QTRH and NSYS, are checked and set aside along the way for reasons that reinforce the daily-chart resistance filter established in the case study earlier in this chapter. QTRH's five-minute pre-market chart showed its pre-market high sitting at only $1.50 by his first read, but on closer inspection this figure needed correcting upward to $1.76, because the daily chart showed the moving-average resistance sitting just above the lower figure — meaning a trade entered at the lower, incorrect level would have run straight into daily resistance almost immediately, whereas the corrected, higher level cleared that resistance. This small correction is instructive in itself: a misread pre-market high is not merely a cosmetic error, because every downstream number in the trade — the entry, the stop, the target — is anchored to it, and an error of even a few tens of cents at that first step propagates through the entire order.

By the time the pre-market screening is complete, four genuine Setup One candidates remain: Tesla, DXCM, ESIO, and W. But rather than placing orders on all four, the instructor makes a deliberate, explicitly stated decision to narrow the field down to only two, on the practical grounds that managing more than two simultaneously triggered positions — each one requiring a stop-to-breakeven adjustment the instant it moves 1R in the trader's favor — becomes genuinely difficult to do well in real time; he recommends, as a general practice, capping simultaneous pre-market orders at two. Of the four candidates, he selects W and ESIO specifically because their price, at the moment of decision, sits closest to their respective pre-market highs — the single deciding factor, by his own account, with no other criterion overriding it.

The two orders are placed using the platform's classic order-entry workstation rather than the hot-button system reserved for the post-open setups, since there is no urgency: a pre-market order can be entered calmly with a minute or two to spare. For ESIO, sized for a $100 planned risk with a twenty-cent stop distance (translating to roughly 500 shares), the buy-stop-limit is entered at $21.76 (one cent above the $21.75 pre-market high he had marked) with a limit at $21.80, a stop loss twenty cents below entry at $21.56, and a profit target forty cents above entry (2R) at roughly $22.16. For W, with a pre-market high marked at $118.28, the buy-stop is entered at $118.29 with a five-cent limit buffer, and — reflecting the wider ATR-derived stop appropriate to a much higher-priced stock — a stop loss set $1.30 away at $116.99, sized down to a much smaller share count, roughly 77 shares, to keep the dollar risk in the same rough $100 band despite the far larger per-share stop distance.

Both orders are transmitted, and the charts are switched to one-minute candles to watch the open. Neither W nor ESIO ends up triggering: the instructor notes ESIO selling off away from its entry level immediately once the bell rings, with no fill; both pre-market orders on his two chosen names are eventually cancelled once it becomes clear neither is running toward the trigger, and — in a detail he flags specifically as a lesson about resistance — he observes that both stocks did eventually claw back up toward their pre-market highs some minutes later and were rejected right at that level exactly as the pre-market-high-as-resistance framework predicts, reinforcing why the stop-to-breakeven discipline matters for any trader who does end up entering nearer that level later in the session.

With both pre-market Setup One attempts cancelled and no fills, the instructor's attention immediately shifts to hunting for Setup Four and Setup Five candidates among the same four favorite names — Tesla, DXCM, ESIO, and W — and finds nothing valid forming on any of them in the minutes right after the open. Rather than forcing a trade onto a name that no longer shows a clean pattern, he returns to his live scanner and finds a fresh name that had not appeared on any earlier list: EXEL. It had already printed a bullish run of higher highs — described as forming a doji-like candle at the top of the move — followed by a first pullback candle. He measures that pullback candle's height at roughly 20–25 cents, clicks the corresponding 20-cent hot button, and places the resulting order at the pullback candle's high; the fill comes in at $21.90, with a stop loss at $21.62 and a profit target at $22.42 — a 28-cent stop against a 52-cent target, close to the standard 2R structure. He flags one specific piece of self-criticism in real time as he takes this trade: the bid-ask spread on EXEL at the moment of entry was unusually wide, on the order of seventy-five to ninety-one cents by his own reading of the screen — far outside the five-to-ten-cent range he otherwise insists on — and he explicitly says that, in retrospect, this was a trade he probably should not have taken purely on spread-discipline grounds, attributing the lapse candidly to a degree of impatience after two failed pre-market attempts. The trade nonetheless worked out: price went sideways for a stretch after the fill, worrying him, before turning upward and eventually clearing the full 1R mark, at which point he shifted the stop loss up to the $21.90 entry price exactly per the standing rule, locking in a risk-free position; price then pulled back and tagged that adjusted stop for a scratch — before reversing yet again and running on to strike the original $22.42 profit target, which he books as a full winner despite the stop-out along the way having already guaranteed a break-even floor.

While EXEL was working, the instructor's attention was also drawn to ETP, which had climbed to the top of his scanner's volume ranking — the single loudest name in the market at that moment. Pulling up its chart, he registers immediate frustration: by the time he looked, ETP had already printed a textbook first-candle Setup Five — a small bullish first one-minute candle closing above the moving averages — and had already run well past it without him. He explicitly narrates what the missed trade would have looked like: buy above the high of that first candle, stop below its low, and a profit target reached, by his own estimate, within about three minutes. Rather than chasing the already-extended move, he waits for the pattern to give him a second chance, and it does: the price pulls back into a lower-high inside bar, which he treats as a valid Setup Four first-pullback entry. He measures the pullback candle, clicks the 25-cent hot button, and the order fills at $23.47, with a stop loss at $23.24 (a 23-cent risk) and a profit target at $23.89 or $23.90 (roughly a 42–43-cent reward, close to 2R). After the fill, price ran in his favor; once it cleared 1R he shifted his stop up to the $23.47 entry price exactly per the rule — but this time the outcome inverted relative to EXEL: price pulled back, tagged the adjusted breakeven stop, and stopped him out flat, at zero, only to reverse yet again afterward and run on to strike the original profit target without him in the position. He is candid that this outcome is simply frustrating rather than avoidable — the stop-to-breakeven rule did exactly what it is designed to do, which is guarantee that a trade which has already earned 1R can never turn into an outright loser, even though on this particular occasion it meant giving up a winner that would otherwise have been captured had the stop never been moved at all.

The session closes with one winning trade (EXEL) and one break-even trade (ETP), against the two Setup One attempts that never triggered at all and cost nothing. The instructor uses this exact mix of outcomes to restate the mathematical core of the entire course: a losing trade costs exactly 1R, a winning trade returns something between 1.5R and 2.5R depending on the target chosen, and a trader who simply nets out to roughly one winner and one loser (or scratch) most days, executed with strict risk discipline, ends up net positive on average — his own illustrative arithmetic being that an average of 1R of net profit per trading day, compounded across twenty trading days in a month, works out to roughly 20R for the month, which translates to a 20% monthly account return if risking 1% of capital per trade, or a 40% monthly return if risking 2% per trade. He closes the lesson, and this stretch of the course, with the reminder that day trading under this framework can be genuinely and consistently profitable — but only for traders who follow the rules exactly as laid out, rather than treating any single element of the process, from the pre-market resistance check to the Level 2 spread filter to the mechanical stop-to-breakeven rule, as optional.

Looking back across the entire session as a single, connected story rather than as four isolated trades, the value of narrating a full day end to end — rather than only the isolated winning examples used earlier in the chapter to illustrate each setup individually — becomes clear. A student who only ever sees curated, successful worked examples for each setup in isolation could reasonably come away believing that a valid Setup One or Setup Two chart pattern is close to a guaranteed winner. This session shows the opposite: on this particular day, two carefully selected, textbook-looking Setup One candidates, chosen specifically because they were closest to their own pre-market highs among four qualifying names, both failed to trigger at all. The entire day's profit came instead from two setups that were not on the original pre-market list — EXEL, a fresh name spotted only on the live scanner after the pre-market plan had already failed, and ETP, a name that had been provisionally earmarked for a post-open setup rather than a pre-market one and was nearly missed entirely. This is also why the instructor treats the live, continuously running scanner as being just as important a tool as the pre-market watchlist itself, rather than as a secondary backup: on this particular day, it was the only source of any trade that actually worked.

The EXEL and ETP trades are also a useful matched pair for illustrating that the stop-to-breakeven rule is symmetric in its consequences — it protects against loss with exactly the same mechanical indifference whether the outcome, in hindsight, turns out to favor the trader or not. On EXEL, price dipped down to tag the adjusted breakeven stop and then reversed to run on to the full profit target, meaning the stop adjustment cost nothing and the trade banked its full 2R-equivalent gain regardless. On ETP, the identical mechanical action — move the stop to entry the instant 1R is banked — produced a scratch at breakeven rather than the full target, because price happened to dip to the adjusted stop before running on to where the original target was waiting. The instructor's point in walking through both outcomes back to back, rather than presenting only the flattering one, is that a trader executing this rule correctly cannot know in advance which of these two outcomes a given trade will produce; the rule is followed identically either way because its purpose is not to maximize the outcome of any single trade but to guarantee that no trade which has already reached 1R in profit can ever be allowed to turn into an outright loser. Over a large enough sample of trading days, this is what converts a strategy with, at best, a roughly even win rate into one that is durably profitable — the losers are capped tightly at exactly 1R, the breakeven scratches cost nothing at all once they have been protected, and the full winners return multiples of what the losers ever cost, which is the entire arithmetic the instructor returns to, again and again, throughout every lesson in this stretch of the course.

Read as a whole, the five setups, the Level 2 filter, and the fully narrated live session together form a single, closed system rather than a menu of unrelated techniques a trader might pick and choose from at will. The three pre-market setups exist to answer one question — where, relative to the pre-market high, should an order be resting before the bell — and they differ from one another only in how far below that high the entry sits and what shape the chart takes on the way there: right at the gate for Setup One, at a lower pivot for Setup Two, at the final candle of an already-formed bull flag for Setup Three. The two post-open setups answer the same underlying question after the bell has already rung and the pre-market plan has either worked or failed, again differing from each other only in whether a flag has to form first (Setup Four) or whether the very first candle is itself the signal (Setup Five). Level 2 reading is not a sixth setup sitting alongside these five; it is the filter and the early-warning system that determines whether a pattern that looks valid on the candlestick chart is also structurally safe to actually execute, given how little room the strategy's tight stop-losses leave for anything to go wrong in the order book underneath. And the live session exists to show, honestly, that on any single day some fraction of the setups identified in advance will simply fail to trigger, that the live scanner will sometimes produce the day's only working trade from a name that was not on the original watchlist at all, and that the stop-to-breakeven rule will sometimes cost a trader a fuller profit it might otherwise have captured — not because the rule failed, but because the rule's entire purpose is to cap the downside on every single trade with the same mechanical certainty, regardless of how any individual trade happens to resolve. It is this combination — a small, closed set of chart patterns tied to the pre-market high, a fixed 1R-based risk unit applied identically to every one of them, an order-book filter layered on top, and an unwavering stop-to-breakeven discipline once 1R is banked — that the instructor is pointing to when he says, at the close of the live session, that day trading under this framework can be genuinely and consistently profitable, provided every one of these pieces is followed exactly as taught rather than treated as optional.
